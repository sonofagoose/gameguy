# Imani, 52

> It's wrong about the blues and right about my choir: it never once let me bend a note, but it
> showed me the gap between my singers and my organist is something to hold, not fix.

### Day 1

My nephew Jalen handed it over grinning: "You'll either love it or write an essay about how it's
wrong." Thursday rehearsal ran long. Kitchen table, good headphones, pencil, the back of a set list.

Black. A drip, close, in my left ear. Low on that palm-sized screen, a bone-white thing in the
reeds, two dark eyes with a glint in each, breathing. I pressed R1 and it hummed, a plain *mm*, and
a ring of pale light ran out over the ground: reeds, a coiled hose, gone. Called again. Far off, a
teal ring answered.

Left stick toward it, click the right stick, and the camera slid into the creature's eyes: a drop
under a spigot, never falling, and between us a fine turning line, a loop that wouldn't close. Hold
R2, let go on the drop's tick. That's a cutoff; I give forty a night. The drop swings, very
slightly, which was the first thing I trusted. The flams tightened, the loop closed, and a low note
bloomed under us both, the ghost you hear when a chord truly locks. I turned the volume down to see
whether my ears were making it. They weren't; the game is. Fine. It's the right note.

"Oh. You're in," said the drop, flatly. It wasn't flat. I checked. I needed my readers for that one
line.

Dawn came up green and the first chord *rang*, thirds sitting low, the way my altos tune them when
the organ lays out. Unaccompanied choirs drift toward pure, and I'd rather mine be in tune with each
other than with the organ. That's my hill.

I held R1 and a twelve-point star bloomed; right stick to the left wedge and the garden went Lydian.
It never said the word. I said it to my kitchen.

Then three little knots, stumbling five against three. Left face button (Y on mine): I pressed early
on purpose, twice, and both times the fork waited for the beat. A drum machine with manners. Pencil:
*QUANTIZED.* When the swell came I let go of R2 early, not trusting it, and the cadence fell home
anyway, plainer than it wanted to be. Water ran. A blue figure sat on the pool's edge for a second.

### Day 3

The mornings climb by pure fifths. Twelve of those overshoot seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma;
I can see the ending from here. Knowing the cadence never spoiled one.

In the Glass Hills nothing holds a third longer than a passing note. Pythagorean thirds are sour;
somebody knew. Doorstop, an iron wedge propping a gate for nobody, says one word a day. Today's was
"Late." Basses.

I held L1 and the voice wheel read *ah, oh, oo*, two dark petals still to come, and I laughed out
loud: it's the warm-up. Ee, eh, ah, oh, oo, bright to dark. My choir sings it every Thursday.

Another swell, and this time I trusted it: a suspension over a pedal, adding voices, sweet, never
sour, mine to hold. The vamp. The director's hand up, the room waiting. I held it till my arms
prickled and dropped it on the one, and the whole ridge rang.

That's when it clicked. It took away my notes and left me my job: who sings, in what color, and
when. I don't sing a note on Sunday either.

### Day 4

Bell Meadow: bell-flowers big as houses, thirds sweet as home, fifths a hair pinched. Meantone. I
held the wheel open and the music kept sinking until the fifth between two bell-flowers wasn't a
pitch anymore but two pulses, three against two, that I could watch. I tapped it out with the
pencil. I have taught the fifth in October and hemiola in March for twenty-six years, and never once
in the same week.

### Day 6

The Highway at two in the morning, tail lights on salt, the Stage under one red bulb, Patio dangling
from it like lights left up after a cookout. If anywhere would let me bend, it was here. I held R2
and pushed the stick, trying to lean up into the third the way every singer I love does. Nothing.
Nothing in this game picks a pitch. Every note it gave me was correct, and under a red bulb I didn't
want correct. I wanted the third that leans and never quite arrives. The driving mood has the flat
seven and none of the grit: a bagpipe tune in sunglasses.

And six days of three-note breaths. Triads. I teach triads to fourteen-year-olds in September.

What saved the week: the gray soldiers break if you strike just behind the beat. A whole career
teaching teenagers to lay back, and finally it's a weapon.

### Week 2

At rehearsal the piano's thirds sounded wide to me. They are wide. I'd only stopped forgiving them.

Ruben, second tenor, sixteen, has the same handheld, game and all. Lunch in the choir room, speakers
on: we both held R1 and pointed at the same station, a four-count played on both, and Ruben clapped
on two, church boy, and nothing happened. On the one, Ruben. Then the games shook hands, his singing
roots and fifths, mine the thirds. Doorstop's bass can't fit in a speaker that size, and my ear
built it anyway. Ten minutes in, the clocks slipped and we flammed like my altos after a key change,
loose, never sour. I clapped us back on the one. Ruben said, "You do that to us every day."

There's no putting a 3.5-inch screen in front of thirty, though.

### Week 3

The vine was at the garden again, blurred and doubled until I tuned to it, then sharp as a pen line.
Its voice sits on the true third, the one no stack of fifths can reach. It hummed yesterday's tune
back with the turn changed, the way my choir brings back last week's alto line, revised by
committee. I didn't correct it. I correct everything.

Jeanne, off Caravan No. 27, traded me a note off the wheel for the one echo I'd kept since the first
morning, as evidence. The note was the seventh partial, lower than any flat seven on my piano, the
one my tenors find when Curtis lays out. It opened the sky, the raw harmonic series, the nearest
this game gets to the blues. It still won't let me lean into a note. But it knows where the note is.

### Week 4

The thirteenth morning. B-sharp: on a piano, C; here a comma high, the green a shade warmer than the
first day's. Everyone was at the garden: the vine, Jeanne, the white cat, the fly on Hum's knee.
Three offers: seal the seam, keep everything pure, or hold the gap with the vine. My hill. I took
the Wolf's.

The lake drained through the crack, and the garden rang purer than anything I've heard from a
machine, and there was no one in it but Hum.

I sat a long time. Then, in pencil: *Pure isn't alone. Pure is forty people listening so hard they
move their own note.* The game has never stood in front of a choir. It may have stood in front of
me.

The river offered the morning again. I turned it off.

### Week 5

Rehearsal. The a cappella verse of "I Need Thee" sagged the way it always does, everyone sweetly in
tune with each other and a comma under the world. Curtis and I have been divorced eleven years, and
he has played every Sunday of them. Usually they snap up to him, or he drags them up a bar at a
time. Tonight I had him come in under our last chord and let the gap sound for one beat, choir and
organ a hair apart, then lifted them into him. He looked at me over the music like I'd been
drinking. Second time, it worked.

Home, and the thirteenth morning again. I held R2 and the vine held its note, two tones almost one,
swelling like a section a breath before it locks, and nobody fixed it. Then a third voice, home,
sounded between us, sung by neither. I didn't turn the volume down to check.

The Mirror Pool went downstream to the ocean. The fourteenth morning was the first that wasn't a
repeat.

### Week 6

Past the lake the lands are tuned by thirds and never come around. I go a little further most
Thursdays.

Sunday's set list, my hand: *out of "I Need Thee," up a fifth into "Blessed Assurance," so it feels
like morning.* Curtis read it back to me. I hadn't noticed I'd written it.

Mailed Jalen his essay, one page, pencil: *It's wrong about the blues. It's right about my choir.
Bring yours at Christmas. I'll take the thirds.*

**Worked:**

- Tuning as geography passed an expert's audit in the first hour (pure thirds at home, no held
  thirds in the Pythagorean hills, pinched fifths in meantone, a comma waiting exactly where twelve
  pure fifths put it), so the game earned her trust instead of asking for it.
- Putting the five choral warm-up vowels round the L1 wheel, and making R2 a swell you hold and
  release on the one, turned the controls into conducting, a skill she already had, which made her
  objection to fixed pitch into the game's premise.
- Slowing the mood wheel until a fifth becomes a three-against-two you can watch taught a veteran
  teacher something new, by joining two facts she had always taught months apart.
- Letting swing break the Greys rewarded the most gospel thing in her hands, playing just behind the
  beat, at the moment the game was frustrating her most.
- The two-console split (roots and fifths on one game, thirds on the other) and a drift that flams
  but never sours made lunch with a student feel like rehearsal, and on the choir-room speaker her
  ear filled in Doorstop's missing root, as the low voices are built to make it.
- The thirteenth morning argued with her deepest conviction instead of flattering it: the Wolf's
  offer let her act on 'pure is right' and feel what it costs, and the duet's few audible seconds of
  comma gave her a technique she took to her real organist.

**Broke:**

- Because the player never picks a pitch, a trained singer can't bend, lean or blue a note, so the
  promise that nothing sounds wrong also means nothing quite sounds like her; it bit hardest at the
  Stage, and the Drive mood's flat seven without grit read to her as 'a bagpipe tune in sunglasses'.
- Six days of three-note breaths felt like her September triads unit: to a gospel musician sevenths
  and ninths are where you start, not a prize, and the slow breath ladder bored her.
- There's no way to show a 3.5-inch screen to a room, so using it in class went no further than one
  student at lunch, and the single line of on-screen text needed her reading glasses.
- The treaty only connects on a clap on the one; a student raised clapping on two and four got
  silence and no hint why.
- The Wolf's ending equates purity with being alone, which contradicts the most communal sound she
  knows, a choir tuning without a keyboard, so she felt accused before she could hear it as an
  argument.

**Wish:**

- Give trained ears a lean: a small, deliberate bend on a held note toward its nearest pure
  neighbour (the blue third, the sky's seventh partial) that always resolves on release, perhaps
  only in fierce, so a singer can be wrong on purpose for a beat without anything turning sour.
- Let the breath ladder listen: a player whose ears prove themselves early (a clean first meeting
  with Drip, cadences released on the one) could earn the seventh and ninth within a day or two
  instead of a week.
- Give a teacher a way to show a room: since a take is only its inputs, a laptop player that replays
  takes from the SD card on a projector would put the slowed fifth and the vine coming into focus in
  front of thirty students.
