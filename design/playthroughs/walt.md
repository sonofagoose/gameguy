# Walt, 71

> **Status, 2026-11-26.** History: an imagined player of the older thirteen-morning design
> (2026-11-13). Not a real player, and not the design now. What the game is: `design/the-game.md`.

> It gave me something to keep time with in the evenings, and at the end it asked me to hold the one
> sound I'd spent fifty years tuning out.

### Day 1

Nate set it up Sunday and showed me the buttons once, fast. I wrote them on an index card in block
capitals, like a setup sheet. Last game I played was Pac-Man at the Elks.

Armchair, TV off, glasses on. Black screen. A drip, close by. Low down, a white thing in some reeds
no bigger than my little fingernail, two dark eyes with a speck of light in each. You can hear it
breathe.

Card says R1, call. It hummed with its mouth shut, like me at red lights, and a thin ring of light
ran out over the ground: reeds, a coiled hose, gone. Nothing. Again. Way off, a ring came back,
blue-green.

I walked toward it on the left stick, which is the size of a shirt button, and the answer kept
coming, muffled and late, like the band in the next ballroom through a hotel partition. Thirty years
of Saturdays I heard that sound.

Card says click the right stick to look close. Three tries; the stick's stiff, and every push swung
the camera at the sky. Then it glided in: a drop hanging under a spigot, and between us a thread of
light I'll take on faith. Hold R2 to sing, let go on the drip's tick, press on the next. That I can
do. That's a drummer. We flammed, then we didn't, and a third note came in under us both. Ray and I
used to get that on shout choruses, a ghost tone under two trumpets. We called it the third man.

“Oh. You're in.” Printed small as a pill-bottle label. The drop fell on my fellow's head and stayed.
Then morning came up green, flowers tall as silos.

At the first knot the left button rings a fork, and the game sets every hit on the beat for you,
which I resented for about a minute. Then I held R2 through a big swell, let go on the one, and it
came home like the last chord of a ballad. Water ran. For a second somebody blue was sitting on the
edge of the pool, then nobody.

Quarter to eleven. First thing I've kept time with since Bev.

### Day 5

I know where I am now, and not from the screen. Walk clockwise round the lake and at every border
one note goes up a half step, a key up a fifth; the other way, one comes down. I count borders like
bars of rest. The land straight across from home is a long walk round and a short boat ride over:
the flat-five sub, D-flat seven for G seven, made into a ferry. Somebody who knows built this.

Hold R1 and the moods sit across the top, bright to dark. Four have woken. Lydian. Ionian.
Mixolydian, every blues I ever played. Dorian at the top, cool as “So What.” It doesn't name them
unless you ask. Good. If it had explained Dorian to me I'd have put it in a drawer.

In the glass hills an iron doorstop holds a gate open for nobody. One word a day. I know the type.
He sings lower than that little speaker can go, and I hear him anyway.

At night the river sings your day back: the board tape in Ray's car after a job, everybody wincing
at their clams. You can keep four takes.

### Day 11

Three nights I thought my eyes had gone. Every third day there's a vine on the fence by the garden
pool, and it was doubled and smeared. I cleaned my glasses and brought the magnifier lamp in from
the bench. Then I sang with it, held R2 on its sway, and it pulled together, one vine, sharp. The
doubling is the game: out of tune shows double. That's a mean trick to play on a man of seventy-one,
and I'll say it once.

It sings the third of the chord, and it sits low, where Ray used to lip his third down so the
section would ring. Nobody ever told us why. With the vine my echoes go warm. Without it they're
open and bright, like a band warming up on fifths.

Then it hummed yesterday's tune back to me, wrong in the bridge. Bev hummed along to everything and
got the bridge wrong, the same wrong way every time, until her way was the way. I turned it off.

It's a vine with flowers. I don't know what kind. Bev would have.

### Day 14

Night lands are mostly black. On the Highway at 2 a.m. I can't see a blessed thing but tail lights,
so I call every two steps like a bat and go by the rings. After a long fight, thumb on the stick and
finger on L2 the whole way, the base of my thumb aches like a day on the Bridgeport. At the river I
pick fierce when tomorrow's a bright land and gentle when it's a dark one, which is about how I
drive now. Gentle never says “easy.” It just widens the pocket.

### Week 3

I kept the take from the day the vine came clear. Lived that day over four times, too, till the path
I walked went grey and the music quit breathing. Tonight at dusk, eleven nights on, it walked out of
my band, my own tune in blue, howled a few seconds, two notes rubbing that wouldn't settle, and went
off south toward the crack.

Nobody warned me. The long-eared girl with the caravan said not to ride my luck, and I thought she
meant the boat.

I know what it means. I'm not stupid. I've had the counsellor and the pastor and my daughter on the
subject, and I don't need it from a toy. Put it in the drawer.

### Week 4

Nine days. Came back because the TV was worse.

Under the bridge the Metronome locks everything to its grid and learns your dodges, like a drum
machine, and I lost a lot of wedding work to drum machines. I laid back on it the way Basie's band
laid back even at full shout, and it came apart. Good to be needed for something I'm good at.

Then the Wolf, down in the crack. It's every echo you wouldn't let go. They come one at a time,
howling, each one a tune of yours with a rub in it, and you sing it home and let it go. The last was
the vine day. I knew it by the bridge. I held R2 on it as long as the breath lasted and let go on
the one. It resolved, and walked into the water.

I won't pretend that was about a game.

### Week 5

Thirteenth morning. Everybody was in the garden. Home came round a hair bright, like the last set,
when the horns are warm and the band's drifted sharp and nobody minds. B-sharp. I've seen B-sharp on
a chart maybe three times, always some arranger showing off, and played it as C. It isn't. Twelve
fifths stack up over, like twelve shims each a hair heavy: you can't hold every one to print and
still close the stack. The piano spreads the error so every key's a little off and none's out of
spec. That's the bridge city. F-sharp and G-flat are both second valve on a trumpet; turns out
they're two notes that agreed to share. The Wolf is what you get when you won't spread it: the whole
stack lands in one joint, and that joint howls.

Three offers. I took the Wolf's: keep it pure. The lake drained through the crack and the music
stayed beautiful, and I was alone in it. I know that one. I listened to the end.

Next night the river offered the morning again. No speech. Just the river.

### Week 6

Took the duet. You and the vine hold the gap between you, and for a few seconds you hear it: two
notes almost one, wah-wah-wah, two horns tuning. Fifty years that sound has sent my hand to the
slide. You don't touch the slide. You hold it. Then a third note came in between us, home, and
neither of us sang it. The pool went downstream to the ocean. Fourteenth morning, the first one that
isn't a repeat. That's the one I'll remember.

Nate came Sunday with his. We held R1 and pointed at the same spot, it counted four, we clapped on
the one, and after that his game sang the thirds and mine the roots. Neither sounded thin. By supper
we'd drifted into flams, and he clapped us back in before I could. Kid's got time.

Got the horn out after he left. Long tones, concert B-flat. My lip quit inside a minute and it
sounded like a truck. I'll do another minute tomorrow.

**Worked:**

- Because sound and picture are one model, Walt had a complete second channel: when the fine lines,
  small text and black night lands failed his eyes, he found his way by counting key changes at
  borders and used the call as a lantern, and lost nothing essential.
- Leaving the theory unnamed until asked let an expert name the modes himself, and building the lake
  as the circle of fifths, with the far shore a short sail across, read to him as the tritone
  substitution: the click that told him the maker knows music, and that earned his patience.
- Rewarding timing on the beat rather than reaction speed (the pocket, the cadence released on the
  one, laying back against the Metronome) turned a lead trumpeter's thirty years of time into an
  advantage at seventy-one instead of a handicap.
- Choosing gentle or fierce each night at the river, with no 'easy' label anywhere, let him set
  difficulty by how well he could see tomorrow's land rather than by pride.
- Letting him take the Wolf's ending without comment, then quietly offering the thirteenth morning
  again, is what brought him back for the duet: the river's silence did what a lecture would have
  ruined.
- The duet's few seconds of comma, slow beating that every horn player knows from tuning up, asked
  him to hold the exact sound his hands had spent fifty years correcting, and it became the moment
  he keeps.

**Broke:**

- The game's picture of being out of tune (doubled, smeared, out-of-register inks) looks exactly
  like failing eyesight, so he spent three nights believing his eyes had got worse; using blur as
  information is a trap for older and low-vision players.
- The one line of text, the one-pixel figure between two voices and the mostly black night lands are
  below what his eyes can make out on a 3.5-inch screen even with reading glasses: he took the first
  figure on faith and walked the Highway by ear.
- Clicking the small, stiff right stick swung the camera before focus engaged (three tries the first
  night), and long fights with the left thumb on the stick and the left index finger holding L2 left
  an arthritic hand aching.
- The eleven-day rule ambushed him: the take he valued most left for the Wolf with no warning he
  could read (Jeanne's line sounded like advice about the boat), so it landed as a sermon on grief
  aimed at a widower; he quit for nine days and came back only because the TV was worse, which no
  design should count on.

**Wish:**

- A seeing option (heavier figure lines, larger high-contrast text, a lifted floor for night
  blacks), plus an out-of-tune cue that never relies on blur alone, such as flicker or draining
  colour, so no one mistakes the game's doubling for their own eyes.
- Let an echo show it is nearing the Wolf a night or two before it goes (a red glint in its eyes at
  the river, a rub creeping into its tune), so letting go is a choice the player sees coming rather
  than a loss that ambushes them.
- Latching lock-on (tap L2 on, tap again off) and a focus click that ignores the stick's nudge for a
  moment, so the game's holds and clicks don't land on stiff hands.
