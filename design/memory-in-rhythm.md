# Memory in rhythm: the world remembers the way a brain does

Brainstorm, saved 2026-11-13 at Morgan's request. Nothing here is decided. It answers his question
asking for creative, joyful and empowering ideas about memory and computation in the phase and
cross-frequency coupling of oscillations. It builds on Part 4 of `shape-3-the-lake-ring.md`
(equal temperament and cross-frequency coupling).

Most of it is true. Brains really do remember and compute with rhythm and phase, so each idea hands
the player a real power. Where the science is argued over, the idea says so.

Each idea starts with the moment a player would feel. Then come how it works in the game, what's
true underneath, and how it must sound. The ear rules from shape 3 (Part 1) hold for every one.

---

## How phase should sound

Phase is *when* something happens within its cycle, not how high it is. So being out of phase
should never be heard as being out of tune:

- **Slightly out of phase** sounds hollow and moving, like a flanger's whoosh. That is what a sound
  mixed with a copy of itself a few milliseconds late does.
- **Further out** sounds loose in time: flams, stumbles, a crowd clapping almost together.
- **Locking in** makes the sound solid and louder (two copies in phase add up), and on screen the
  inks come into register (`look.md`, direction 7).
- **Exactly opposite** cancels. The same note half a cycle late is silence, which is how
  noise-cancelling headphones work.

That keeps the music in tune while phase does its work, and it is physically exact: what you hear
is what is happening.

## Remembering

### 1. The river sings your day back

- **The moment.** The river carries you home at night, and your day comes back to you as a quick
  little song made of your own melodies. After a big win it plays backwards from the win, and you
  hear exactly which moves earned it.
- **In the game.**
  - The night ride home (shape 2's river) replays the day, fast, as a song built from what you sang
    and struck, with ripples running down the water in time.
  - This is where you choose which takes to keep.
  - By day, the Remember mood (shape 3, Part 2) is the same power: rewind things, call your echoes.
- **Underneath.**
  - During rest and sleep, the hippocampus (the brain's memory centre) replays the day's paths many
    times faster than they happened, in short bursts called ripples.
  - Awake at a reward, it often replays the path backwards from the reward (Foster and Wilson 2006).
  - Only the backwards replays grow when the reward grows (Ambrose, Pfeiffer and Foster 2016). One
    reading is that they tie the route to the prize.
- **How it sounds.**
  - Faster, never higher: the notes are packed onto the night's beat, not sped up like tape.
  - Backwards means the notes in reverse order, each still sung forwards (a retrograde, as composers
    use it), so it's still a tune.
  - All of it is in the night's key.

### 2. Dreams find shortcuts

- **The moment.** You wake knowing a way you've never walked, or humming a tune you never played.
- **In the game.**
  - Some nights the replay joins two places you visited separately, and at dawn the new way is
    there in the world, traced in sun-print blue.
  - Or you wake humming a melody you never played, recombined from the day's motifs: a new song for
    the songbook.
- **Underneath.**
  - Replay sometimes stitches together paths the animal never took (Gupta and colleagues 2010).
  - People who slept after practising a puzzle were more than twice as likely to discover its hidden
    shortcut as people who stayed awake (Wagner and colleagues 2004, "Sleep inspires insight").
  - A later study using other kinds of puzzles found no such effect (Schönauer and colleagues 2018).
- **How it sounds.** The new melody is made only of your own motifs, in the coming day's key, so it
  sounds like yours.

### 3. Sleep makes you better

- **The moment.** The move that kept slipping away yesterday comes easily this morning, and you can
  feel the difference.
- **In the game.**
  - Whatever you practised today is a little easier tomorrow: wider timing windows, a smoother feel.
  - That is progress without grind. The game rewards rest.
  - The night's music is the three nested rhythms of deep sleep: a slow swell, a shimmer riding
    each swell's crest, and sparkles in the shimmer's dips.
- **Underneath.**
  - Recordings inside the human hippocampus (Staresina and colleagues 2015, twelve patients) found
    three rhythms nested in deep sleep:
    - spindles (12–16 a second) rise on the up-swing of slow waves (under about once a second);
    - ripples (80–100 a second) sit in the spindles' troughs.
  - That nesting is thought to carry the day's memories into long-term storage.
  - Separately, people who practised a finger-tapping sequence were about 20% faster after a night's
    sleep, and not after the same hours awake (Walker and colleagues 2002).
  - Later work argues much of that gain came from how it was measured (Rickard and colleagues 2008),
    and it's still argued over. The game treats it as a gentle truth, not a number.
- **How it sounds.**
  - The nesting is a lullaby, all drawn from the night's chord:
    - the swell is a slow breath of the whole chord;
    - the shimmer is a soft tremolo on its crest;
    - the sparkles are high bell notes in the shimmer's dips.

### 4. Pools remember

- **The moment.** You hum to a still pool and it shows you what happened beside it.
- **In the game.**
  - Still water keeps what happened near it as rings within rings.
  - Sing the pool its own note and the past plays across the surface in sun-print blue (`look.md`):
    who passed, what was lost, where someone sat.
  - Knowledge is the treasure, as in Outer Wilds.
  - It's shape 1's Capture turned around: the world kept a phrase of you.
  - Morgan's worksheet already says it: the pool of water is a mirror, it wants to return to the
    ocean, and it "knows something about: TIME".
- **Underneath.**
  - A hologram stores a scene as the interference pattern between light from the scene and a plain
    reference wave. Lit by the same reference wave, it gives the scene back (Gabor 1948).
  - The scene is spread over the whole plate, so a broken piece still shows all of it, only less
    sharply. A puddle splashed from the pool could hold the whole memory, blurred.
- **How it sounds.** Your note is the reference wave, so it has to be the pool's own note. The past
  comes back softened and in tune with you.

### 5. The lake is a memory that circles

- **The moment.** A song you sang at dawn comes back across the water at noon, just when you need
  it.
- **In the game.**
  - A song sung on the shore travels round the lake and comes back across the water minutes later.
  - Only what's sung again stays; the rest fades.
  - Sing a Lift at dawn and it reaches the far shore at noon, when you're there and need it. You're
    sending help to your future self.
  - The lake is already the circle (shape 3, Part 3). This makes it the circle's memory.
- **Underneath.**
  - Early computers such as EDSAC (1949) and UNIVAC I (1951) kept their memory as pulses of sound
    circling in tubes of mercury.
  - At the far end each pulse train was read, cleaned up and sent round again. Anything not re-sent
    faded.
- **How it sounds.**
  - A returning song arrives in tune with now: if the day's key has moved, the lake brings it back
    in the new key, as echoes do (shape 3's rule).
  - Its age shows as softer and lower, never sourer.
  - It lands on the beat.

### 6. One breath holds seven notes

- **The moment.** The first time a whole seven-note phrase fits in one breath, and it works.
- **In the game.**
  - A song-spell is one breath long.
  - At first a breath holds three notes, a chord's worth. Each note more extends the chord (a
    seventh, a ninth, and on) up to seven, which is every note of the key. That is noodles' ladder
    from shape 3's teaching table.
  - Then you learn to nest breaths into bars and bars into phrases, the way musicians remember long
    pieces.
  - It's one answer to shape 1's open question, "one breath for everything".
- **Underneath.**
  - One model says about seven fast brain cycles (gamma) fit inside one slow one (theta), which
    might be why short-term memory holds about seven things (Lisman and Idiart 1995).
  - It's contested: many estimates put the limit nearer four (Cowan 2001).
  - Grouping things into chunks is how memory stretches past the limit (Miller 1956). That part is
    solid.
- **How it sounds.**
  - The notes are sung one after another, or voiced the way pianists voice big chords, leaving out
    the note that would clash. They're never stacked into a block of all seven, which is mud.
  - A breath that runs out ends on a held, fading note, not a cut.

## Computing

### 7. Things in time are one thing

- **The moment.** You hum a pulse, and the loose parts around you start moving with you as one
  machine.
- **In the game.**
  - To join things, set them pulsing in time with you. Everything on the same beat moves as one.
  - Nudge a part off the beat and it falls away.
  - Parts in 3-against-2 make machines that walk.
  - This puts physics under shape 1's Bind and Loops and shape 3's Build mood: binding is
    synchrony, and you can see the shared pulse.
- **Underneath.**
  - An old, still-debated theory says the brain binds an object's features (its colour, shape and
    movement) by making the neurons that sense them fire in synchrony (von der Malsburg 1981; Singer
    and Gray 1995).
  - Critics argue the evidence is thin (Shadlen and Movshon 1999).
- **How it sounds.**
  - Each bound part sounds one note of the chord, so a machine is a chord in rhythm.
  - A part slipping off the beat flams (two hits a hair apart) before it falls away. It's never a
    wrong note.

### 8. Bits in the beat

- **The moment.** The old machine you just woke is thinking, and you made it think with a rhythm.
- **In the game.**
  - The world's old machines think in rhythm: a hum on the beat is 1, a hum on the off-beat is 0,
    and three hums vote.
  - Curious players can build working logic out of song, like Minecraft's redstone circuits for
    musicians:
    - tie one input to "off" and the vote is AND;
    - tie it to "on" and it's OR;
    - shifting a hum by half a beat is NOT.
  - It's an optional deep layer, never the main path.
- **Underneath.**
  - The parametron (Eiichi Goto, 1954) stored each bit as the phase of an oscillator. It rings at
    half the frequency of the signal driving it and settles into one of two phases half a cycle
    apart: 0 or 1.
  - It computed by majority vote: three inputs go in and the output takes the majority's phase.
  - Japan built working computers from them. The PC-1 at the University of Tokyo, finished in
    1958, used about 4,200.
- **How it sounds.** A 1 is a hit on the beat and a 0 a hit on the "and" between beats. Both are in
  time, so a running machine is a groove, and you can hear it think.

### 9. The gears of time

- **The moment.** You realise the bells are a clock, and you know when the gate will open before it
  does.
- **In the game.**
  - The world has bells and tides on different cycles. Where you are in the big cycle is the
    combination of where each small one is.
  - A three-day life and a four-day life meet every twelfth day.
  - Learn to read the bells and you can predict when things line up: a lock that opens only when
    three pendulums swing together, the day someone's cycle meets yours.
  - It's foresight, and it quietly teaches polyrhythm (shape 3's "lives that meet every twelve
    days").
- **Underneath.**
  - The brain's map of space (grid cells) is laid out in modules whose scales step by about 1.4,
    close to √2 (Stensola and colleagues 2012; other data vary more).
  - √2 happens to be the equal-tempered tritone.
  - Theorists propose that the modules work together like gears of different sizes: each alone
    repeats and is ambiguous, but read together they pin down a position (Fiete, Burak and Brookings
    2008).
  - It's the arithmetic of the Chinese remainder theorem: cycles of three and four meet every
    twelve; add a five and they meet every sixty.
- **How it sounds.**
  - The bells are tuned to one chord, or to a five-note (pentatonic) scale, so any overlap sounds
    good.
  - The moment they all strike together is the full chord. The alignment is the reward.

### 10. Previews at the fork

- **The moment.** You stop where the path splits, listen, and hear where each way goes before you
  choose.
- **In the game.** Pause at a fork and each path is sung ahead of you in a quick phrase, one then
  the other: its key, its mood, any danger. It's intuition you can hear.
- **Underneath.**
  - As a rat runs, each theta cycle (about eight a second) sweeps the brain's map through the places
    just ahead.
  - At a fork the sweeps run down one branch and then the other, as if trying each (Johnson and
    Redish 2007).
  - Later recordings found the brain switching between the two possible futures cycle by cycle,
    about eight times a second, more strongly near the fork (Kay and colleagues 2020).
- **How it sounds.**
  - Each preview reaches its path's key through a chord the two keys share (a pivot chord), so even
    a far key sounds like a doorway, not a clash.
  - Danger is told by mood (a darker mode, a lower voice), not by sourness.

## Power

### 11. Small pushes, huge swings

- **The moment.** Something tiny moves something enormous, because it found the rhythm.
- **In the game.**
  - A seed-sized creature moves giants: a bell-flower, a fallen log, a hanging bridge, a sleeping
    beast.
  - Find its rhythm, push on the beat, and the swing grows until it rings, rolls, or carries you
    across a gap.
  - Pushing against its rhythm takes the swing out, so the same skill calms things.
  - The timing window is generous, and near-misses still push, just less: here timing *is* the
    skill.
  - Tiny but mighty: timing is the power.
- **Underneath.**
  - Anything that swings has its own rhythm. Pushes in time with it add up, so small pushes build a
    huge swing, and pushes against it take energy out.
  - In English bell towers, one ringer on one rope swings a bell that can weigh more than a tonne
    right round, mouth up to mouth up, by pulling at the right moment of each swing.
- **How it sounds.**
  - The bell-flower is tuned into the key. A real church bell carries a minor third in its sound,
    which clashes in a major key. A made bell can be given any overtones, so this one's sit inside
    the current chord.
  - Each push adds a soft beat.
  - As the swing grows, more overtones bloom: a crescendo into a huge chord.

### 12. Lead by rhythm, not force

- **The moment.** You walk steadily, and one by one the creatures fall into step behind you until
  there's a parade.
- **In the game.**
  - Creatures fall into step with your pulse.
  - A crowd in step can do what none could alone: sway a bridge across a gap, rock a boulder loose
    (idea 11, with many small pushers).
  - It's shape 2's "hearts falling into step", out in the world.
- **Underneath.**
  - The heart's pacemaker leads because it's the fastest steady rhythm; the rest of the heart
    follows.
  - When London's Millennium Bridge opened in 2000, walkers fell into step with its slight sideways
    sway, and their stepping made the sway grow (Strogatz and colleagues 2005).
- **How it sounds.**
  - Each creature that joins adds a voice or a drum to your groove, in key.
  - Before they lock in they're loose in time, a little chattery, never out of tune.

### 13. The downbeat

- **The moment.** You clap, and everything around you snaps to the same moment, like an orchestra
  on the conductor's downbeat.
- **In the game.**
  - A sharp clap resets everything near you to the same point in its cycle:
    - enemies' attack cycles line up, so one dodge beats them all;
    - machines restart together;
    - a scattered choir falls into unison.
  - It builds on shape 1's "in the pocket".
- **Underneath.**
  - A sudden, strong input can reset an ongoing rhythm so it starts again from that moment.
  - Body clocks reset to morning light, and brain rhythms can reset to sudden events. In monkeys,
    even a touch resets the rhythm of the hearing cortex, so the sounds that follow land at its
    most receptive moment (Lakatos and colleagues 2007).
- **How it sounds.** The clap has no pitch. The instant after it, the music restarts on the one:
  the drop.

### 14. Talk by getting in phase

- **The moment.** A stranger's mumble turns into words the moment you breathe with them.
- **In the game.**
  - Strangers, creatures and places mumble until you match their pulse and breath. Then their words
    come clear.
  - It's how you'd meet the voices (shape 1) and the beloved (shape 2).
- **Underneath.**
  - One theory says brain areas pass messages best when their rhythms line up, so one is listening
    at the moments the other speaks ("communication through coherence"; Fries 2015).
  - When people listen to speech, their slow brain rhythms lock to its syllables (Giraud and Poeppel
    2012; shape 3, Part 4).
  - A listener's brain activity follows a storyteller's, and the more closely it follows, the more
    of the story they understood (Stephens, Silbert and Hasson 2010).
- **How it sounds.**
  - Out of phase is muffled and smeared in time, like a voice through a wall, never out of tune.
  - As you lock in, the words sharpen and the melody of their speech comes through in key.

### 15. The build-up is yours to land

- **The moment.** The music swells, you hold it there as long as you dare, and you choose the moment
  it drops.
- **In the game.**
  - The world tells you a peak is coming (the swell, the rising pulse), and you decide when it
    lands.
  - Hold the suspended note and release it for the drop: a finisher, a huge ring, the lake
    resolving.
  - The wheel's hold-and-release becomes the same gesture as shape 1's finishing cadence. Holding is
    anticipation, and releasing is arrival.
- **Underneath.**
  - In a small study (eight listeners), dopamine was released in one brain region (the caudate) in
    the seconds before their chills, and in another (the nucleus accumbens) at the peak itself
    (Salimpoor and colleagues 2011).
  - Anticipation and arrival each have their own reward.
- **How it sounds.**
  - The held moment is tension sweet enough to hold: a suspended chord, or the dominant over a held
    bass note. Never a sour note.
  - Held longer, it keeps building rather than souring.
  - It can't be held forever: the breath runs out (idea 6), so the drop always comes.

## The frame: the world remembers the way a brain does

- **The day** is the slow cycle, with your moments nested inside it (shape 3's ladder of rhythms).
- **The night river** is sleep: replay, shortcuts, getting better (ideas 1–3).
- **The lake** is a memory that circles (5).
- **The pools** hold the past (4).
- **The bells** are the map (9).
- **Your breath** is how much one moment can hold (6).

Morgan's Generative Self loop (notice, capture, extract, select, sing) fits inside it: you notice by
day and learn by night. Over the game, the player learns to think in rhythm.

## What to test first

1. **Small pushes, huge swings, in the meadow.** One giant bell-flower: the creature hums its rhythm
   and pushes on the beat until it rings. It's the most immediate joy and power here, and simple to
   build.
   - Does timing feel like power in the hands?
   - Does the build into a huge chord sound good to Morgan?
2. **The river sings your day back,** together with shape 2's echo test. Play a minute, then hear it
   sung back fast, and after a win, backwards from the win.
   - Does your day as a song feel like yours?
   - Can you hear which moves earned the win?

## Not yet connected

Claude's notes on where these ideas don't fit together yet. They are not decisions.

- **The night's length.** A day is meant to last fifteen to twenty minutes (shape 2), so the replay
  has to stay short, and maybe skippable.
- **What sleep improves.** Which skills get easier overnight, by how much, and how to make sure
  nobody feels forced to sleep to make progress.
- **Placed or found.** Whether dream shortcuts are placed by hand (safe for level design) or found
  by recombining the day (surprising, but it has to stay musical).
- **The lake's level.** If the lake is a memory, does a low lake forget? Songs would fade before
  they came round, and bringing the water back would bring them home. That would make wasatch's
  lake rule and idea 5 one thing.
- **Breath as progression.** Whether a breath growing from three notes to seven is the main way the
  player gets stronger, and how breath is shared with running and climbing.
- **The downbeat's cost.** A clap that lines up every enemy is strong. It needs a cost or a wait so
  it isn't a panic button.
- **How deep the logic goes.** The parametron layer is optional; how much the world teaches it is
  open.
- **Reading the bells without a screen.** How the player learns the world's cycles with nothing on
  screen.
- **Two consoles.** Getting in phase (14) and leading by rhythm (12) are natural between two
  players. What happens when the consoles can't hear each other is shape 1's open question.
