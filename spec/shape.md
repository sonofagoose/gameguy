# The shape of the lands

> **Status, 2026-11-26.** History and material, from the specification of 2026-11-19: the shape of
> each land and what its ground wears. The garden's covers in the code come from it. Not placed in
> the direction beyond the garden. What the game is: `design/the-game.md`.

_Part of the specification (`../SPEC.md`, section 12). Morgan, 2026-11-19: "I wonder how the sky and
it's colors and it's inhabitants and the topological shape of the different lands might be ideally
experienced considering the powerful laws of the world", and of the first proof field: "the whole
surrounded-by-giant-blades-of-grass-that-are-all-basically-the-same thing that's happening right
now... should not be a common thing... different lands have different types of foliage and different
relationships to the player". This file decides the shape of the whole world, the shape of each of
the twelve lands and why it is that shape, what each land's ground wears and what that does to the
player, and how the shapes make sure something always calls. `world.md` has each land's table;
`sky.md` has what is overhead; `size.md` has the sizes._

## The shape of the whole

The world is not a ring. It is the shape that pure intervals make, and a person can walk every part
of it.

- **Fifths go round.** Twelve lands stand on the shore of a round lake, each a pure fifth from its
  neighbours: the sharp keys climb clockwise from the Garden to the Seam, and the flat keys fall
  counter-clockwise from the Garden to the Seam. Read clockwise from the Seam's flat side (D♭),
  through the Garden, to its sharp side (F♯), every land is a pure fifth above the one before.
  Twelve pure fifths do not come back to where they started: they overshoot by a comma. So the shore
  is not a circle. It is one turn of a spiral that climbs 1.96 m going clockwise from the Seam's D♭
  side, through the Garden, to its F♯ side, and its two ends meet at the Seam with a step between
  them.
- **The climb is too gentle to see.** It is 16 cm for each land: a hair, over just under a kilometre
  of shore. Nobody notices it until the Seam, where one side of the crack stands 1.96 m above the
  other. A person standing at the foot of the step looks up at the high side's edge, about twice
  their own height above them, and hears the two sides beat.
- **The city closes it by agreement.** The Seam's bridge crosses the step in twelve equal treads of
  16.3 cm, one for each street. That is equal temperament, built: the error shared out so evenly
  that nobody on the bridge feels a step at all. Everywhere else in the world the ground is true and
  the step is real.
- **Thirds go out.** Behind each land, away from the lake, lies the land a pure third above it, and
  behind that one, another. These are the lands past the lake (`world.md`). The land a third out
  from the Garden is an E, and it is not the Highway's E: it is 21.5 hundredths of a semitone lower,
  so the two stand apart in the sky as a double star (`sky.md`). Three thirds out, the note has
  nearly come round to where it began and missed, as the fifths do. So the rings going outward are a
  spiral too.
- **Overtones go up.** Over the lake the day's note stands in the air as rings of light, each at the
  height of one of its overtones (`sky.md`). Height is the octave everywhere, and an octave is 100
  m. The seventh ring is dark: sevenths are the way the caravan comes, and the way up.
- **The mirror goes down.** Under the Mirror Pool the same ladder runs downward, and every chord is
  turned over (`world.md`, "The deep").
- **So there are four ways to travel that are not walking:** round the lake by a fifth, each
  morning; outward by a third, after the ending; up by the rings; down by the pool. Each is a real
  interval, and each arrives somewhere a person can stand.

**What a person sees of it.** From the crown of an aster: the lake, ringed by twelve lands in their
twelve colours, each with its one silhouette (below). Over the water, the hoops of the day's note.
After the ending: a second ring of lights beyond the first, and a third beyond that.

**A cheat would be:** a round map with twelve themed zones and a locked gate at the Seam; "past the
lake" as a list of bonus levels.

## Height

- **Height is the octave, and an octave is 100 m.** A land's ground lies between its lowest note and
  its highest. So a land with a wide range has tall relief, and a land that holds one note is flat.
- **Low ground holds low things.** A land is laid out so that what stands low is low in register:
  the long ridges and big bells low, the small bells high. A thing keeps its own note wherever it is
  moved (`laws.md`, the families). Low voices move the ground and high ones the air (`../SPEC.md`,
  9), so valleys are where heavy things are moved and ridges are where you fly.
- **A semitone is 8.3 m and a whole tone 16.7 m.** Where a land is built in steps, the steps are
  these.
- **The lake is the lowest open ground in the first turn,** and the mouth of the horn. Only the
  Seam's crack, where it drains, and the deep under the Mirror Pool go lower.

## How a land is found

The best open worlds learned this the hard way. One of them first laid its discoveries along roads
between tall towers, and found that four players in five walked the roads as if on "an invisible,
but obvious guide rope", while the rest wandered at random. Its makers threw that plan away. What
they built in its place is the craft this section uses (the sources are at the end). None of that
game's content is used.

- **Something calls from everywhere, in light and in sound.** From the middle of every piece of
  every land, at Hum's height, something not yet found is in sight or in earshot (`promises.md`). In
  this world a landmark can call round a corner and in the dark, because it sounds: its phrase
  arrives as a ripple of light crossing the ground from its side (law 6), as an answered call does
  (`../SPEC.md`, 6), so the way to it is seen even when it is hidden. Nothing depends on telling
  direction by ear: the speaker is mono.
- **Each kind of thing calls in its own way,** so a person learns what they are walking toward:

| what | how it is seen from far | how it is heard from far |
|---|---|---|
| a great instrument | the largest shape in its land | its own low note, under everything in that land |
| a life | a small light that moves on its round | its tune, a phrase at a time, stopping where it always stops |
| a wonder | the thing itself happening, when wind or rain or a passer-by sets it off | the same |
| a pool | a still mirror: the only water that shows the stars and the sky's rings whole; wet stone and puddles mirror only the lights near them | silence: a pool is the quietest place near it |
| a pack | doubled outlines at dusk | a howl that beats |
| a river | a line of sparks | water, growing as you come nearer |
| something that flies | its figure against your home (`sky.md`) | the interval its note falls by as it passes |

- **Tall things hide what is behind them.** Stalks, ridges, walls and leaves are black shapes. So
  from any one place only two or three things call, and the rest are hidden until you move. Coming
  round a stalk or over a crest shows the next ones.
- **Every tall thing has something at its top:** a crown of bells, a view, a ring of the sky. The
  eye goes to the top, and the feet follow.
- **Going to one thing shows two or three more.** The score tool counts it: along the straight line
  from any thing to the next nearest, at least two others come into view.
- **No rope.** Paths exist: the hose, an ant trail, the road. Most of what is worth finding is not
  on them. The check is a person's own take: if more than half of anyone's steps in a land fall on
  its paths, the land is leading them, and its landmarks are moved.
- **Night changes what calls.** At 2am the things that sound at night are the only lights, so the
  same land sends a person somewhere else.
- **Nothing counts for you.** The songbook shows what has been found and never how much is left.
  There is no number out of 144 anywhere.

**A cheat would be:** markers on a map; a compass arrow; a trail of collectibles.

## What the ground wears

Every land has three to five **covers**: what grows or lies on its ground. No cover is the main
cover of two lands. And no cover is scenery: each one does something to Hum, or for Hum, by a law.
Crossing a land is learning what its ground does.

- **A cover changes as you walk.** No more than about two hundred metres go by under one cover.
- **A cover is the real thing, at one to a hundred,** and is drawn as itself.
- **A cover is heard.** Each has its own sound underfoot, and that sound obeys the land's tuning and
  metre.
- **The ground remembers through its cover** (below): moss keeps a footprint for a day, dust for
  years, glass not at all.

## The twelve lands

Each: its shape and why; how sound behaves in it; what is seen; the one silhouette it shows across
the lake; what its ground wears; and how it treats the hands.

### The Garden: a bowl

- **Shape.** A shallow bowl a kilometre and a half across, with the Mirror Pool at its lowest point.
  The ground rises gently from the pool on every side to a rim of raised beds, each edged with a
  wall of brick ten metres high. The spigot stands on the highest rise at the back, and the hose
  runs from it down across the whole bowl to the pool.
- **Why.** A bowl gathers. Every sound made in it runs down to the water and comes back up, so the
  Garden is the land where your own voice returns to you from all sides. It shows all seven laws
  gently because nothing in a bowl is far away.
- **Sound.** A call from the rim is heard everywhere in it. The bowl has one low note of its own, a
  C, which the right call at the pool's edge wakes.
- **Seen.** The asters' skyline; the spigot, a monument of brass; the hose, a green river you can
  hear.
- **Silhouette.** A low bowl of towers, with a glint of brass among them.
- **Hands.** Easy. Gentle slopes, short distances, everything rings.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| lawn | blades of grass, 4 to 9 m | bends away as you pass and springs back in your wake, shedding dew that lands a moment later: your path is heard just after you (travel) |
| moss | cushions a metre high | swallows sound. On moss your call goes half as far and you are dim: the place to rest and the place to hide (cancelling) |
| clover | three leaves on a thread of a stalk, 3 to 7 m up | a roof. Each leaf is a drum skin: rain plays it, and when you hum under one its figure shows on its underside (standing waves) |
| bare soil | crumbs as boulders, and puddles | puddles are mirrors, and each can be crossed on its still lines when you hum its note |
| leaf fall | last year's leaves, 5 to 10 m, curled | each is a bar with its own note. A dry leaf rattles when you step on it and tips like a seesaw |

### The Alfalfa: a field in furrows

- **Shape.** Long straight furrows, 75 m from ridge to ridge, with a row of alfalfa on every ridge:
  a forest in corridors. Along a furrow you walk easily for a kilometre. Across them you climb a
  ridge, push through trunks and step over strings, every 75 m.
- **Why.** A furrow is a line, and this land shows travel along a line and sympathy along strings.
  Sound, like you, goes easily along a row and hardly at all across.
- **Sound.** A flick sent down a row comes back from its end, upside down if the end is tied. The
  whole field sways in three, and its wave can be seen crossing the canopy.
- **Seen.** From inside, a corridor to a point of light. From above, stripes, with a wave of light
  crossing them.
- **Silhouette.** One level line 80 m up, rippling.
- **Hands.** Rhythm. Walk a row in step with the field's sway and the field passes your step along:
  you cover ground at twice the pace. Against it you wade.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| the rows | alfalfa, 60 to 100 m, strung with spider silk and baling twine at every height | a string you brush sounds, and so does every string tied to it. You climb by them, and a careless crossing plays a chord (sympathy) |
| stubble | cut stalks, 5 to 20 m: open pipes of every length | wind plays them in rows. Running a row plays its scale. A stalk you stop with a pebble drops an octave |
| chaff | loose husks in drifts | every step hisses. It has no note, so it hides your voice from whatever hunts by pitch |
| ditch bank | wet clay | holds a footprint, and a flick sent along the water runs ahead of you (travel) |

### The Glass Hills: ridges that are bars

- **Shape.** Long ridges of broken bottle glass. Each has a sharp crest and smooth curved faces,
  because a shard is a piece of a cylinder: every slope is half a pipe. Between ridges lie valleys
  of marbles. The ridges are of every length, and the longer lie lower.
- **Why.** A ridge of glass is a bar, and a bar's note follows its length. This land shows building
  up, and a bar that rings for half a minute is where one push after another adds.
- **Sound.** Every step on a crest rings the whole ridge. A curved face gathers sound to a line:
  stand on that line and you hear the far side of the valley as if it were beside you.
- **Seen.** Hard hair-thin lines and glints, under the sharpest stars in the world.
- **Silhouette.** A row of glints along the water's edge.
- **Hands.** Loud and committed. You cannot walk a crest in silence, and a curved face is a slide:
  once on it you are going down.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| shards | broken bottle, crests 5 to 30 m high | ring underfoot for half a minute; walking a crest is playing its bar. Faces are slides |
| marbles | glass balls, 1.6 m | roll when pushed in time (building up). A call through one comes out the far side as a beam |
| ground glass | sand | soft and quiet. When a ridge hums, the sand on it gathers into the ridge's figure, so the sand tells you the note (standing waves) |
| frost | plates of ice over everything till the sun reaches them | breaks with a tick at every step, in the land's long silence. Melting, it is the land's river |

### The Bell Meadow: a floor that swings

- **Shape.** Round hills and round hollows in an even pattern, 150 m from crest to crest, like a
  sheet shaken once and stopped. Bell-flowers as big as houses nod over it on stalks 30 to 60 m
  high. Between the hills are level rings of clover where the lives dance.
- **Why.** Everything here is a pendulum: the stalks, the bells, the ground's own swell. It shows
  building up and falling into step.
- **Sound.** Bells hang at every height, some at Hum's. Brushing a stalk rings its bells. A crossing
  made out of step is a jangle; made in step, in the meadow's six, it is the dance.
- **Seen.** Lanterns nodding in two tempos, three against two.
- **Silhouette.** A line of hanging lights that sway.
- **Hands.** Dancing. Jumping, pushing and stepping on the beat all go further.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| bell-flower stands | stalks you brush, bells of 2 to 5 m | you can't cross without ringing. Cross on the beat and what you ring is the tune |
| grass in seed | seed heads on long stems | rattles. They keep the tempo of whoever last passed for a few bars (falling into step), so a path remembers a pace |
| ribbon | strips left from the wedding | flutter on the beat: the tempo read by eye. They lift in any wind and can be ridden a little way |
| dance rings | clover, cropped close | springy. A jump on the beat goes twice as high (building up) |

### The Highway: a line

- **Shape.** Dead flat to the horizon: salt and tarmac, one straight road with a railway and a
  picket fence beside it, and one pole. A real roadside pole is nine to twelve metres. Here it is a
  kilometre tall, thirty metres through, and its wires go off into the sky. It is the tallest thing
  in the world and it is seen from every land.
- **Why.** This land is distance. It holds one note, so it has no height. It shows travel, and
  travel needs room.
- **Sound.** Almost nothing answers: tarmac and salt take in very little. A call goes out and is not
  heard again, unless it meets the one wall that sends it back, seconds later. The wires on the pole
  hum the wind's note across the whole land. Whatever passes falls in pitch as it goes by.
- **Seen.** The widest sky; a vanishing point; red only in found light: tail lights, the Stage's one
  red bulb, and the sun's own figure on the fifth morning; the Stage, one bulb.
- **Silhouette.** One line straight up into the stars.
- **Hands.** Fast, straight and exposed. Nothing slows a sprint: tarmac is the fastest ground in the
  world. There is nowhere to hide and nothing to climb but the pole and the fence.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| tarmac | stones a metre across set in black | takes no note and gives none. The fastest ground in the world |
| the painted line | a dash 15 m wide and 300 m long, full of glass beads | sends light straight back where it came from. On it your own call returns to you, and you are seen from everywhere |
| salt | cubes 20 to 50 cm | every step is a click with no note. Running on it is a shuffle: rhythm with no pitch |
| cracks | weeds in a split in the road | small gardens in the Garden's own key: the only shelter, and the only things that answer |
| rubber | black marks left by tyres | soft and silent. A strip of nothing, to cross unheard |

### The Chrome Nave: a room

- **Shape.** The inside of a parked car: a hall 250 m long and 120 m high. At one end the
  windscreen, a wall of glass. Under it the dashboard, a cliff with the radio's dial across its
  face. Two seats, each a mesa full of springs. A floor of carpet, and under the seats, the dark.
- **Why.** It is the only land that is wholly a room, walled and roofed end to end, so it is the
  land where sound stays. A room has its own low notes, and places where each one vanishes. It shows
  falling into step, because everything in a room hears everything else.
- **Sound.** Clap between the two side windows and the clap comes back again and again, about every
  five seconds, softer each time: a looper as wide as the car (law 6; the width, about 150 m, is an
  estimate). Walk the length of the hall on one low note and it swells and dies and swells: the
  room's standing wave, under your feet.
- **Seen.** Every light twice, in chrome. The sky only through glass.
- **Silhouette.** A long low dome with one shining edge.
- **Hands.** Bouncing and timing. The machines hit as evenly as they can, and you move with them.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| carpet | loops of fibre, an arcade a metre high | muffles and slows. Nothing rings on it, and your light goes a short way |
| chrome and glass | mirror | every sound back at once, every light doubled, and no grip |
| dust | drifts, undisturbed for years | keeps every footprint for good. Here the ground's memory is deepest, and you are the first mark on it |
| springs | coils 3 m across, inside the seats | each has its own rate. Land on one in time and it throws you (building up); a forest of them falls into step |
| lost things | a coin 2 m across, a key, a sock | a coin is a gong. Each lost thing is somebody's (`people.md`) |

### The Seam: a stair nobody feels

- **Shape.** A crack in concrete, 150 m wide and deep past seeing, where the lake drains away. A
  bridge across it carries the one city. The ground on one side of the crack is 1.96 m higher than
  on the other. The city's twelve streets run along the crack, side by side across the bridge, each
  16.3 cm higher than the last, so whoever crosses the bridge climbs the whole step in twelve treads
  nobody feels.
- **Why.** Here the spiral's two ends meet and do not join. The city is the agreement that joins
  them: every step made a little wrong so that all twelve are the same.
- **Sound.** Everything beats a little, and every wheel in the city turns on that beating. It is the
  busiest sound in the world, in a four that is dead straight.
- **Seen.** The only lights in rows. Lines drawn in stairs. Wheels turning everywhere at the rates
  the laws give.
- **Silhouette.** Hammers and clock towers over a dark gap.
- **Hands.** Crowds and straight lines. Walking on the grid or off it is a choice you make with
  every step.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| paving | slabs in runs of twelve, each about an eighteenth shorter than the last, so the seams fall closer and closer like frets and each run ends at half the length it began (`world.md`, the Seam's fourth wonder) | step on the seams and you are walking the city's own lines; between them you swing. Greys walk only on ground that has set grey (`people.md`), not on the seams |
| lichen | slow maps of colour on old concrete | the oldest living things here. They change only across years (`years.md`) |
| felt | offcuts from the Piano's hammers | soft and silent, in strips: the city's quiet streets |
| gratings | bars over the drop | each bar is a note, and wind comes up through them from the deep |

### The Aster Steps: a scale you climb

- **Shape.** Terraces up a hillside, each riser one step of the scale: 16.7 m for a whole tone and
  8.3 m for a half, so seven terraces are an octave and a hundred metres. The steps are a little
  uneven, each its own size, as a well-tempered scale is. On the top terrace, under a dome of glass,
  is a copy of your own garden.
- **Why.** A scale is a stair. This land shows cancelling: under the dome is a place with no sound
  in it at all, and the climb to it is a lament, one note at a time.
- **Sound.** Each terrace is one note, and standing on it puts that note under you. Inside the dome
  a call makes no ring.
- **Seen.** Panes of glass over still things. Seeds falling in threes. No wind.
- **Silhouette.** A stepped hill with a glass dome.
- **Hands.** Slow and careful. Climbing is singing a scale, and coming down is singing it back.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| panes | cold frames over the beds | you walk on glass above things that don't move. Your light shows them and they don't answer |
| aster down | seed fluff, in deep drifts | lies still until you sing, then rises on your note and carries you one terrace (gravity) |
| fallen petals | petals of the bell-headed asters | each gives one soft note, once, and then lies quiet till tomorrow: a tune you can play one time a day |
| dry stalks | last year's asters | hollow and dark. They are asters that no longer ring |

### Verbena Hollow: a bottle

- **Shape.** A bowl sunk below the shore, with one narrow mouth toward the lake. Violet thickets
  fill it, 60 to 120 m high, tangled with hawthorn. Along its border runs the wolf's valley, a
  ravine.
- **Why.** A hollow with a narrow mouth is a bottle, and a bottle has one note. Wind across the
  mouth sounds the whole land. It shows sympathy between three: inside one great resonator, two
  voices that can't close a chord are closed by the land itself.
- **Sound.** Soft. Petals swallow everything but your own voice. When the wind crosses the mouth the
  Hollow hums its one low A♭, and everything in it leans toward that note.
- **Seen.** From the lake, only the mouth: a dark notch with violet light in it. It is the land you
  look into, not at.
- **Silhouette.** A gap.
- **Hands.** Quiet and close. Climbing to balconies, slipping past, singing up to a window.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| petal fall | violet drifts, deep and soft | swallows every sound but yours. You move unheard |
| thorns | a hawthorn's thorns among the verbena, a metre to five long (real ones 1 to 5 cm; to check) | pluck as you pass, each a note of the Music Box's comb. One that catches you lets go when you sing its note (sympathy) |
| stems | verbena, square in section | four flat faces and four corners to climb by. The flower heads at the top are balconies |
| the mouth | bare rock at the pass, in wind | pushes back at you like a hand when the Hollow is sounding |

### The Rain Steps: a stair of delays

- **Shape.** Terraces going down, wet, with a gutter along each that spills to the next. Mushrooms
  stand on them, caps 3 to 10 m across. A cloud rests on the upper steps. Under the lowest is the
  Cistern.
- **Why.** What is sung into a gutter rides the running water as a ripple, slowly, and is given back
  where the gutter spills. This land shows travel: what was sent comes back.
- **Sound.** Sing into a gutter and the phrase comes out where it spills, seconds later. Each
  terrace returns you later than the one above, so a walk down the steps is a round with yourself.
  Rain plays the caps.
- **Seen.** A cloud on the ground. Sparks where rain lands. Lights and their mirrors in wet stone.
- **Silhouette.** Steps going down under a cloud.
- **Hands.** Listening. You do something, and wait, and it comes back.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| wet stone | a film of water | a call's ring goes twice as far on it, and every light has a mirror |
| puddles | one in every step, each a different size | each has its own note and its own still lines to cross by |
| caps | mushrooms, 3 to 10 m | drums overhead, and springs underfoot: a small one throws you |
| threads | Puffball's, under everything | step on one and somewhere far off a cap lights and puffs. The ground is wired, and it gossips |
| gutters | channels of running water | a delay. Whatever goes in comes out later, lower and softer |

### The Reed Mouths: a river that halves

- **Shape.** A delta at low water. The river splits in two, and each branch in two again, each half
  as long as the one before, down to the lake. Reeds stand along the channels in ranks, 150 to 300 m
  tall, the tallest by the widest water. Its ground is the lowest of any shore, level with the
  water.
- **Why.** A reed is a pipe, and pipes in ranks that halve in length are an organ. Channels that
  halve are octaves. The land shows a crowd finding one note.
- **Sound.** Wind across thousands of open reed tops is one drone. Frogs sing in rounds that all
  land on one. Mud holds each step and lets it go with a pop, whose note is the size of the
  footprint.
- **Seen.** Organ pipes, graded. Mist. Columns of midges over what is worth finding (`sky.md`).
- **Silhouette.** Pipes in a row, tallest in the middle.
- **Hands.** Steady and slow. You hold a note for a long time and things gather to it.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| reed stands | pipes 150 to 300 m | your voice among them is one pipe in thousands. You can hide a note there, or get it back an octave down |
| mud | wet, deep | slow. Stand still and you sink to the knee: staying has a weight |
| duckweed | a green floor over water | holds only while you keep moving. Walking it is keeping a pulse; stop and you are in the water |
| down | cattail fluff | floats, and carries whoever catches it downwind |

### The Porch: a guitar's top

- **Shape.** A wooden floor raised on three steps, each a cliff 18 m high. Boards run its length, 14
  m wide, with gaps between them of half a metre to a metre and the dark underneath. A chair, still
  rocking. Chimes hanging from the eave. A guitar leaning in the corner. A lamp. Across a narrow
  water, the Garden.
- **Why.** A floor of boards over a hollow is a soundboard over a box: the whole land is the top of
  an instrument. It shows all seven laws again, quietly, because it is the evening before home.
- **Sound.** Warm and short. Every board is a bar with its own note, the longer the lower, so
  crossing the boards is playing them. The space under the floor answers everything a moment late.
- **Seen.** The lamp, humming its one note. The chair's shadow swinging, slow, in two. Leaves coming
  down, each turning at its own rate. The Garden's lights across the water.
- **Silhouette.** A level line, a post, a lamp, and a shape that rocks.
- **Hands.** Unhurried. Everything you have learned, once more, with nothing chasing you.

| its ground wears | the real thing, times a hundred | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| boards | planks 14 m wide | each is a bar. Grain is a furrow to follow; a knot is harder and higher |
| gaps | half a metre to a metre, with wind from under the house | each sounds a note as the wind crosses its edge. They are jumped, or dropped through |
| paint | curled flakes | tip like seesaws and clatter: the land's percussion |
| cobweb | in the corners | strings, at the end as at the beginning. It holds a note you sing into it and gives it back when touched |
| leaves | coming down all evening, 5 to 10 m | rafts in the air. Each can be ridden to the floor |

## No layer is a copy

The sky, the deep and the lands past the lake are not the ring again in other colours.

- **The deep is the world turned over,** and turning a thing over makes a different thing: a life's
  tune sung downward is a different tune, and the life that sings it has a different want.
- **A land past the lake is made from its three numbers** (`world.md`), and its shape follows its
  largest: fifths make ridges and bars, thirds make bowls and water, sevenths make flats, salt and
  smoke.
- **Each is owed everything a land is owed** (`promises.md`): something calls from everywhere; every
  piece is seen; every sound has a yes.

## What stays, and what the land keeps

- **What you build stays where you built it.** A string stretched between two stalks is still there
  next year, a little slacker.
- **What you wear into the ground stays** as long as the lake is full (`size.md`).
- **Each cover keeps a footprint its own length of time:** glass not at all, lawn a breath, moss a
  day, mud till the next rain, dust for years. What is bent, broken or worn by many passings stays
  (`size.md`), and the light the ground keeps follows the lake, not the cover.

## Decided here

| question | decided | why |
|---|---|---|
| What shape is the world? | A spiral shore of fifths, thirds going outward, overtones going up, the mirror going down | it is what the atlas already says in pieces ("fifths go round the lake, thirds go out from it, sevenths go where the caravan comes from"), put together and given to the feet |
| How does the city cross the step? | Twelve equal treads of 16.3 cm | equal temperament is the comma shared equally. A person can measure it with a ruler |
| Where does each land's shape come from? | From what it is made of and the law it shows best | a land designed to look different is a theme. A land shaped by its own sound is a place |
| How does the ground differ from land to land? | Three to five covers each, none shared as a land's main cover, each doing something by a law | Morgan: different foliage, and different relationships to the player |
| How are things found? | By what calls, hidden and shown by tall things; never by a path, a marker or a count | what the makers of the best open world found when they tracked their own players |
| How tall is the Highway's pole? | A kilometre | a real pole, times a hundred. One thing seen from every land is worth more than a skyline of them |
