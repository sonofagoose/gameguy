# The world

_Part of the specification (`../SPEC.md`, section 12): the atlas. Every land, what it is made of,
its great instrument and its twelve wonders; then the lake, the city, the sky, the deep and what
lies past the thirteenth morning. Each land's lives are in `people.md`. A land's score, written when
its stage is built, may add to this but not take away._

## The shape

- **A round lake with twelve lands on its rim,** in the order of the circle of fifths: the Garden at
  the north, sharp keys warming clockwise, flat keys cooling the other way, meeting at the Seam in
  the south.
- **A garden after a storm, from a seed's height.** The lands are a yard, a field, a road, a parked
  car and a porch, each as big as a country to Hum. Asters are as tall as cathedrals.
- **One continuous place.** There are no loading doors between lands. From any shore you see every
  land's lights across the water and hear the far ones late.
- **One to a hundred.** Hum stands one metre, a centimetre of the real garden, and every thing is its
  real size times a hundred (`size.md`).
- **Sizes** (`size.md`): the lake is 3.7 km across, and its shore more than a day's run round. Each
  land is about 3 square kilometres: a kilometre of shore, two kilometres deep. The ring is 36
  square kilometres of ground, and the whole world 7.7 km across.
- **Height is the octave.** Higher ground is a higher register. The rim climbs by a hair all the way
  round, so at the Seam one side of the crack stands a step above the other: the comma, as a ledge.
  The city bridges it.
- **Every place has two faces.** At noon everything sounds at once, quietly, and the land is lit
  from the horizon. At 2am the field is black and you see by calling. The same things are there at
  both hours and do different things.

## What every land has

A key and a morning; a tuning; a material that sets its physics; the law it shows best; a metre; a
reason its people sing; a tradition the songbook credits; a river to free, which raises the lake;
one thing it lends when its note is borrowed; a great instrument to go inside; eight lives; twelve
wonders.

A **wonder** is one true thing about sound, found by doing it. It needs no fight and no words.
Finding one adds a page to the songbook, drawn by the beam.

A **great instrument** is a place you go inside. Each has a trial for Fierce players and can be sat
with on Gentle. Setting it right frees the land's river.

The credits are from general knowledge and are to be checked against sources before the songbook is
written.

## 1. The Garden (C, the first morning and the thirteenth)

Asters, reeds, the Mirror Pool, a brass spigot and a knotted hose. Home.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | pure |
| made of | water and stems: pools, the hose, bell-headed asters, reeds |
| shows best | all seven laws, gently, one at a time |
| metre | four, with Drip's swing |
| why they sing | the morning song |
| the songbook credits | the pure tuning of people singing with no instruments, set down by Ptolemy and by Zarlino |
| its river | the hose's water, which fills the Mirror Pool |
| lends | everything rings, and asters come up through whatever ground you are on |
| new here | Drip; wonder and sunny home; stand where they meet; the first close; the river |

**Its great instrument: the Hose.** A pipe stopped by its own knot.

- Inside, its standing wave is the floor plan: still points are shelter, and bellies blow you down
  the pipe.
- Kinks change its length, and its length changes which points are still.
- At its heart the Great Knot ties five against seven. They meet every thirty-fifth beat, and that
  is the one you clap.
- Opened, the hose sounds an octave higher and every overtone comes back.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The answering field.** Ring one aster and every aster kin to it answers across the garden.
   (Sympathy.)
2. **The seed that follows.** Put home one wedge under a seed's note and it comes to you. (Gravity.)
3. **The parade.** Walk steadily and the pill bugs fall in behind. (Falling into step.)
4. **The puddle bridge.** Hum a pool's note and walk its still lines. (Standing waves.)
5. **The quiet ball.** Sing a sound's mirror and it is gone. (Cancelling.)
6. **The far shore, late.** Call across the water and count until the answer. (Travel.)
7. **The swing.** Push an aster on its own beat until it rings. (Building up.)
8. **The hose's octave.** Stopped, a pipe sounds an octave lower and hollow: it has only its odd
   overtones.
9. **The drip's secret.** A falling drop makes no sound. The plink is the bubble it traps, ringing.
10. **The third man.** Two voices lock, and a lower one sounds that neither is singing.
11. **Three against two.** Hold the wheel on two drips a fifth apart and count them.
12. **The pot.** A hollow has one note, and a bigger hollow has a lower one.

## 2. The Alfalfa (G, the second morning)

A forest of alfalfa. Strings of spider silk and baling twine between the stalks.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | pure |
| made of | strings and stalks; wind |
| shows best | sympathy along strings; travel along a line |
| metre | three: the field sways in a wave |
| why they sing | the work song |
| the songbook credits | the string divided by Pythagoras, and in China the rule of taking away and adding a third of a pipe's length |
| its river | the irrigation ditch |
| lends | wind: everything strung starts to sing |
| new here | Box Elder; drive |

**Its great instrument: the Wind Harp.** Strings between stalks, played by the wind.

- Move a string's bridge and its note moves: halfway is an octave, two thirds a fifth.
- Tune the strings to a chord the wind's own note belongs to and the harp plays itself.
- On Fierce the wind rises while you tune, and the strings have to be caught on its beat.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The bridge.** Stop a string halfway for the octave, at two thirds for the fifth, at three
   quarters for the fourth.
2. **The light touch.** Touch a ringing string at a half, a third or a quarter, and only that
   overtone is left.
3. **Where you pluck.** Pluck a string in the middle and every even overtone is missing.
4. **The running knot.** A flick runs along a stem and comes back upside down from a tied end, the
   right way up from a free one.
5. **Passing through.** Two flicks from opposite ends pass through each other and go on unchanged.
6. **The skipping rope.** Shake a stem at the right rate and it stands in one belly, then two, then
   three.
7. **Tight and loose.** Tighten a string and it rises: four times the pull for an octave.
8. **The heavy string.** A strand hung with dew sounds lower.
9. **The wind's note.** Wind sings on a wire at a pitch set by its speed and the wire's width.
10. **The wave in the field.** March into the alfalfa and the field passes your step along.
11. **The cup and string.** A sound along a taut strand arrives before the same sound through the
    air.
12. **The click.** The shorter a sound, the less it has a note at all.

## 3. The Glass Hills (D, the third morning)

Ridges of bottle glass, marbles, a jar with a lid, rods and bowls.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | pure fifths |
| made of | glass: bars, bowls, bottles. It rings longer than anything, and it breaks |
| shows best | building up; fifths that clamp and thirds that shake |
| metre | none: long tones over an open fifth |
| why they sing | the vigil |
| the songbook credits | tuning by fifths alone, as chant and the oldest written music used it |
| its river | meltwater from the frost |
| lends | a ringing ridge: every strike lasts half a minute |
| new here | Doorstop; cool soul; home sets, and the first borrow is home |

**Its great instrument: the Armonica.** Glass bowls nested on one spindle, turning in water.

- Run round a rim and the bowl sings.
- Here fifths clamp and thirds shake, so a fifth holds a bowl still while you climb it and a third
  shakes the next one loose.
- At the top is the bowl that holds the river. Hold its note until it gives.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The glass that gives.** Hold a glass's own note and it breaks.
2. **The rubbed rim.** Circle a glass's rim and it sings, by sticking and slipping.
3. **Water in the glass.** The fuller the glass, the lower its note.
4. **The still places.** A rod rings when held a little under a quarter of the way from each end,
   and dies when held in the middle.
5. **Out of tune with itself.** A bar's overtones are not whole multiples, which is why glass sounds
   like glass.
6. **The bottle choir.** Blow across bottles of different sizes: one note each.
7. **The wah.** Two glasses a hair apart beat, and the figure between them turns.
8. **Shake, not turn.** Thirds here beat sixteen times a second: sand on a pane creeps.
9. **The lens.** A curved wall gathers a whisper to one spot far away.
10. **The long ring.** A touch stops a ringing glass at once: soft things take sound away.
11. **Fifths that stand.** Here a fifth's figure stands still and a third's shimmers.
12. **Cold glass.** At night the pipes and bottles sound flat, and the morning warms them sharp.

## 4. The Bell Meadow (A, the fourth morning)

Bell-flowers as big as houses on stalks that sway.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | sweet thirds |
| made of | bells and pendulums |
| shows best | building up; falling into step |
| metre | six, danced as three against two |
| why they sing | the wedding dance |
| the songbook credits | the tuning with sweet thirds written down by Pietro Aron in 1523, and the change ringing of English bell towers |
| its river | the mill-race |
| lends | a bell-flower's swing: one great slow push |
| new here | tenderness; the caravan; the wolves' note rises; the first pack; the first walk of three |

**Its great instrument: the Belfry.** A tower of bells and ropes.

- Each bell is swung up by small pulls at the right moment.
- The stair turns when the bells are rung in changes: every order once, none twice.
- The top bell carries a sad third. Give it this land's sweet one and the mill-race runs.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **One rope.** Small pulls in time swing a bell right round.
2. **The sad third.** A bell carries a minor third inside its own sound.
3. **The note that isn't there.** Take away a bell's lowest sounds and its note is still heard: the
   ear builds it from the upper ones.
4. **Long and short.** A longer stalk swings slower: four times as long for half the rate.
5. **Two on one beam.** Two bell-flowers on one stem fall into step, swinging opposite ways.
6. **The swing that walks.** Tie two bells with a thread and swing one: it stops, the other swings,
   and back.
7. **Rounds.** Ring five bells in every order without repeating one.
8. **The wolf's note.** One fifth in this tuning is far too wide, and it howls.
9. **Sweet thirds.** Here a third's figure stands still and a fifth's turns slowly.
10. **Bells meet.** Bells on three, four and five beats strike together every sixtieth.
11. **The warble.** A bell not quite round has two notes a hair apart, and the ringing place wanders
    round it.
12. **The mallet.** Struck with something soft, a bell gives only its low notes; with something
    hard, all of them.

## 5. The Highway (E, the fifth morning)

Salt flats at 2am. Red only in tail lights. Wires on poles. The Stage, under one red bulb.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | sweet thirds, and the notes between |
| made of | wire, tarmac and salt; distance |
| shows best | travel |
| metre | twelve, shuffled |
| why they sing | the blues at 2am |
| the songbook credits | the blues, and the blue notes that sit between a piano's keys |
| its river | the old inflow of the salt lake |
| lends | 2am: the field goes black and every voice is its own lantern |
| new here | Patio; fierceness; the vine sings the third under your cadence; the lean; the first echo wall |

**Its great instrument: the Looper.** The Stage's pedalboard, as big as a town block.

- Each room records what you do in it and plays it in the next, so you play each room against your
  own last one.
- Four rooms are four tracks. The last room is the drop.
- It is a round, written by walking through it.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The passing note.** The black train goes by and its note falls.
2. **The road that plays.** Run a grooved stretch at the right speed and it sings a tune; faster is
   higher.
3. **The fence.** Run past a picket fence and the clicks become a note.
4. **The rail.** Ear to the wire: the train is in the wire long before it is in the air.
5. **Count the thunder.** Light, then sound: the gap is the distance.
6. **The shell.** Hold a bottle to the road's far rumble and it sounds like the ocean.
7. **Wires in the wind.** They hum by themselves.
8. **Two tail lights.** Two blinkers a hair apart drift into step and out again.
9. **The wall across the flats.** Your phrase comes back bars later, on the beat.
10. **The howl.** A sound fed back into itself holds one note for as long as you let it.
11. **The fifths that survive.** Through the Stage's torn horn, fifths stay clean and thirds turn to
    mud.
12. **Far and low.** Far thunder is only rumble: distance takes the high notes first.

## 6. The Chrome Nave (B, the sixth morning)

The inside of a parked car, as big as a cathedral: a dashboard, a mirror, a radio dial, vents,
springs.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | well-tempered: every key its own colour |
| made of | chrome, springs, cones and relays: machines |
| shows best | falling into step, and thinking with it |
| metre | the machines' own: hits spread as evenly as they will go |
| why they sing | the hymn |
| the songbook credits | Werckmeister's temperaments of 1691, and the organ |
| its river | the channel under the windscreen |
| lends | a machine's steady pulse that everything near falls in with |
| new here | the old machines; the turn; letters, heard on the radio |

**Its great instrument: the Organ under the hood.** Pipes, bellows, and an action that thinks.

- Pull stops to build one voice out of plain pipes: each stop adds an overtone.
- Set the firing order and the bellows walk.
- Its deepest rooms think in rhythm: a hum on the beat is a one, off it a nought, and three hums
  vote. They are there for whoever wants them.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The needle.** The speedometer marks the speeds where a passing note bends by a clean interval.
2. **The dial.** Between stations, hiss. On one, a clear voice: a friend's day, or one of your own.
3. **Wiper and blinker.** Three against two, then drifting, then together again.
4. **The cabin's boom.** In a corner the bass booms; in the middle it is gone.
5. **The spring.** Pluck a seat spring and its high notes arrive first.
6. **The cone.** A speaker's cone is a skin moved by a coil. Sing at it and it moves the coil: it is
   a microphone too.
7. **The siren.** A spinning disc of holes: faster is higher, and two rings of holes, three to two,
   play a fifth.
8. **The vote.** Three hums in, the majority out: a machine that thinks.
9. **The wheel that stands still.** Under a flickering light a spinning hubcap stops, or runs
   backwards.
10. **Every key a colour.** One phrase in two keys at the border bell.
11. **The relay.** Clicks speed up into a buzz and then into a note, at about twenty a second.
12. **The roll.** A punched strip that plays itself: the first program.

## 7. The Seam (F♯ and G♭, the seventh morning)

The one city, built on a bridge over the crack where the lake drains. Hammers, felt, wire, clocks.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | equal |
| made of | everything, a little off and all connected |
| shows best | beating that turns: every wheel in the city runs on it |
| metre | four, dead straight, until someone swings it |
| why they sing | the market and the march |
| the songbook credits | equal temperament, worked out by Zhu Zaiyu in 1584 and by Simon Stevin soon after; the piano; the guitar's frets |
| its river | the lake's way out, under the bridge |
| lends | a way through: for one phrase every key is next door |
| new here | the Metronome; the ferry; the Wolf in the crack; trading fours with the busker |

**Its great instrument: the Piano.** You go inside it.

- Hammers, dampers, and three strings to every note, a hair apart on purpose.
- Lift the dampers and sing: the whole hall answers your vowel.
- Under the soundboard hangs the Metronome. It locks the hall to its grid and learns you. Swing
  breaks it.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The stair that never ends.** A note that rises for ever and never gets higher.
2. **The beat that never arrives.** A rhythm that speeds up for ever.
3. **Every figure turns.** Here each fifth turns once a second and each third shimmers.
4. **The paving.** Each stone is about an eighteenth shorter than the last: the rule that makes
   every key the same.
5. **Three strings to a note.** A hair apart, they sing longer and fuller than one.
6. **The pedal.** Sing into a piano with its dampers up and it answers in your own vowel.
7. **The stretched octave.** The top is tuned sharp and the bottom flat, because stiff strings'
   overtones run sharp.
8. **The clock shop.** Clocks on one shelf fall into step.
9. **The quiet square.** Where two streets' noise meets half a cycle apart, it is silent.
10. **F sharp and G flat.** Two bells, one each side of the bridge, agreed to be one note. Pure,
    they are a comma apart.
11. **The busker's drone.** One low held note makes every other note mean something.
12. **The grid.** Snap a swung tune to the grid and it goes grey; loosen it and the colour returns.

## 8. The Aster Steps (D♭, the eighth morning)

Terraces of the garden's own flower, on the far side of the world. On the top one, under a dome,
your garden.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | well-tempered |
| made of | panes of glass over things that don't move |
| shows best | cancelling: a place with no sound in it at all |
| metre | stillness, and then seeds falling in threes |
| why they sing | the lament |
| the songbook credits | the well temperaments again, heard from their dark side |
| its river | the cascade down the terraces |
| lends | stillness: for one phrase nothing near you moves |
| new here | Break: your voice cracks, and opens; the garden's note comes back, doubled |

**Its great instrument: the Bell Jar.** The dome over the copy of your garden.

- Nothing sounds inside it, so nothing shines: you are dark and unheard, and the copy is exact.
- From outside, your note and the dome's are a comma apart. They beat three and a half times a
  second, which is the rate at which the dome rocks on its seat.
- Hold the note and it rocks itself off. Inside, the asters turn out to be real. They have gone to
  seed.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The bell in the jar.** A ringing bell under the dome can't be heard: sound needs something to
   travel through.
2. **The chirping stair.** Clap at the terraces and the echo comes back as a falling chirp.
3. **The pane's figure.** Sand on a pane gathers into a figure for each note you sing it.
4. **The crack.** A voice has two ways of singing and a break between them.
5. **The half step.** The roughest two notes can be: any closer and they start to become one.
6. **The exact copy.** It makes no sound and has no depth.
7. **Falling in threes.** Seeds land three to your two all the way down.
8. **The dome's rock.** A slow beat at a thing's own slow rate can move what a note alone can't.
9. **The green you know.** Two asters on one note, a comma apart.
10. **The shadow.** Behind the dome only low notes arrive: they bend round and high ones don't.
11. **Loud is not double.** Ten asters ringing sound only about twice as loud as one.
12. **The hidden note.** A loud note hides a soft one near it in pitch. Move the soft one away and
    it is back.

## 9. Verbena Hollow (A♭, the ninth morning)

Violet thickets, thorns, and at their edge the wolf's valley.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | sweet thirds, with the wolf's fifth lying along its border |
| made of | petals and thorns: soft things that swallow sound, and tines that are plucked |
| shows best | sympathy between three: what two can't close, a third can |
| metre | six, counted two ways at once |
| why they sing | the serenade |
| the songbook credits | the wolf of the sweet-thirds tuning; plucked tines, from the thumb pianos of Africa to the music box |
| its river | the spring in the hollow |
| lends | hush: soft ground that swallows every sound but yours |
| new here | a chord is a triangle; the close that misses; the vine across the pack |

**Its great instrument: the Music Box.** A pinned drum and a comb of thorns.

- It plays your own tune as the vine remembers it, a little wrong, with pins missing.
- Each pin is a note at a moment. Move a pin and the tune changes.
- Set it right, or set it the vine's way. Either opens the spring.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The thicket's hush.** Deep in the petals only low notes live: soft things take the high ones
   first.
2. **The tine.** A thorn half as long sounds two octaves higher.
3. **The buzz.** A loose husk on a tine adds a buzz that carries further than the note.
4. **The triangle.** Your own four fifths make a harsh third. Someone else's third is sweet.
5. **The close that misses.** One fifth here is a third of a half step too wide, and nothing lands
   on it.
6. **Two ways to count six.** In twos, then in threes, then both.
7. **Near is loud.** Twice as far is a quarter as loud.
8. **The mouth.** Hold a tine to a hollow petal and the hollow picks which overtone sings.
9. **The round.** A tune that fits against itself, started two bars late.
10. **A third below.** The vine hums your tune a third lower, and every note of it still fits.
11. **First to arrive.** Of two calls at once, you turn to the one that reached you first.
12. **The pinned wheel.** A wheel with thorns for pins plucks tines as it turns.

## 10. The Rain Steps (E♭, the tenth morning)

Terraces where clouds rest. Mushrooms, gutters, puddles.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | sweet thirds |
| made of | drops, puddles, caps and gutters; delay |
| shows best | travel: what was sent comes back |
| metre | rain's own, which the ear turns into fours |
| why they sing | the remembering |
| the songbook credits | to be chosen when the land is built |
| its river | the one the cloud is holding |
| lends | rain |
| new here | Puffball; the amen, held; what you let go, come back |

**Its great instrument: the Cistern.** A room under the steps where a sound lasts half a minute.

- Sing a tune slowly and it becomes a chord, because every note is still there.
- The cloud above wants one chord. Build it from a tune.
- Then breathe with the cloud, four bars in and four out, and let go on the one. It rains.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **Big drops, small drops.** Big ones plunk low and small ones tick high.
2. **The babble.** A stream's voice is thousands of bubbles ringing.
3. **The cap.** Strike a mushroom cap in the middle for a thud, at the edge for a ring.
4. **The long room.** Where a sound lasts long enough, a tune becomes a chord.
5. **The downspout.** A longer pipe sings a lower note.
6. **Tick, tock.** The drips all sound the same, and you hear them in pairs anyway.
7. **The spore map.** Puffball's spores hang as held notes: a map you can hear.
8. **The wire underground.** Tap here and hear it there, bars later.
9. **Sung again.** A puddle keeps a phrase only if you sing it again when it comes back.
10. **The puddle's page.** Every puddle has its own figure for every note.
11. **Thunder's roll.** One crack and a long rumble: the far end of the spark arrives late.
12. **The bow.** Light is sound forty octaves up, and the eye joins the bow's two ends with a colour
    that isn't in it.

## 11. The Reed Mouths (B♭, the eleventh morning)

A delta at low water. Thousands of reeds, frogs, mud.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | pure fifths, over a drone |
| made of | reeds and breath |
| shows best | falling into step, in pitch: a crowd finding one note |
| metre | long rounds that all land on one |
| why they sing | the chant |
| the songbook credits | drones under song, from the tanpura to the bagpipe; the sheng and the mouth organs that came from it |
| its river | the delta's mouths |
| lends | a ground: one low note under everything |
| new here | be the ground; staying; your band fights for you |

**Its great instrument: the Mouth Organ.** A marsh of reeds on one wind-chest.

- It sounds breathing out and breathing in. Out gives one chord and in gives the other: home, and
  the chord that leans on it.
- So breathing is the cadence, and a slow, even breath opens the delta one mouth at a time.
- On Fierce three winds push against the beat, each harder than the last.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The reed.** Alone it squawks. A pipe tames it into a note.
2. **The twelfth.** Blow a stopped pipe harder and it jumps an octave and a fifth, not an octave.
3. **Blow and draw.** A mouth organ sounds breathing out and breathing in, with a different chord
   each way.
4. **The bend.** Draw hard on a free reed and its note sinks.
5. **Pipes in a row.** Cut a pipe to two thirds and it sounds a fifth higher.
6. **One voice, two notes.** Shape the mouth and a whistle rises out of a drone.
7. **The frogs take turns.** Neighbours call in each other's gaps.
8. **The crickets' thermometer.** They chirp faster when it is warm.
9. **The path that sinks.** Walk the small round path singing each step pure. One lap, and the whole
   place is a comma lower.
10. **The ground.** One low note under everything makes every reed mean something.
11. **Forty move one.** A crowd comes into tune by everyone moving a little.
12. **The brood.** Something here wakes only every thirteenth morning, and its cousin every
    seventeenth.

## 12. The Porch (F, the twelfth morning)

Leaves coming down beside a porch with its light on. A chair, still rocking. Across a narrow water,
the garden.

| | |
|---|---|
| tuning | pure |
| made of | wood: boards, a chair, chimes, a guitar in the corner |
| shows best | all seven laws again, quietly |
| metre | the chair's slow two |
| why they sing | the porch song: the evening before |
| the songbook credits | the five-note scales that wind chimes and half the world's songs are made of |
| its river | the narrow water between here and the garden |
| lends | evening: everything leans one wedge toward home |
| new here | the oldest echo at the door; the half close; the night that doesn't end |

**Its great instrument: the Guitar.** You go in by the sound hole.

- The body is one great bottle, the top is a pane with its figures, and the six strings are a sky
  above you.
- They are tuned in fourths and one third, and tuned pure they miss each other by a comma. There is
  nothing to defeat here. Tune it as well as it can be tuned.
- Then the porch song is played, with whoever is in the pool by the chair.

**Twelve wonders.**

1. **The guitar in the corner.** Sing near it and it hums your note back, and only yours.
2. **The strings that don't agree.** Tune a guitar string by string, each pair pure, and the last
   comes out a comma flat of the first.
3. **The rocking chair.** The smallest push at the right moment.
4. **The chimes.** Five tubes with no half step among them: any order sounds good.
5. **The screen door's spring.** Its high notes arrive first.
6. **The board that rings.** Held at its still places, a porch board is a bar.
7. **The sound hole.** Blow across it: the body's one low note.
8. **The lamp's hum.** The porch light hums the note of the wires, and it is not on the wheel.
9. **The amen.** Every dawn in this world is yesterday settling into today.
10. **The half close.** A tune that stops on the chord before home, and waits.
11. **The jar of lights.** Fireflies in a jar fall into step.
12. **The stairs.** Every step has its note: a staircase is a scale.

## The lake

- **Sailing.** The flowerpot from the first evening is the boat. The wind is the day's own note,
  blowing from the day's land. The sail takes hold by law 2: with home on the wind's note you sit
  still, a fifth off you run fast, and straight across you are thrown back. So you tack by moving
  home.
- **The ferry.** From the Garden straight across to the Seam: the far side of the circle is secretly
  next door. A water boatman rows it.
- **The level is the world's memory.** Low, phrases end on lovely almost-endings, songs sent over
  the water fade before they come round, and trails of light die fast. Each freed river raises it.
  Full, the music comes home.
- **The lake's slow wave.** A song sung on the shore at dawn comes back across the water at noon,
  softer and lower, in today's tuning.
- **The tide.** The moon pulls the level a little (below).
- **The Shimmer.** A bronze grove standing in the water, its bars tuned in pairs a few beats a
  second apart on purpose. It is the one place where wobble is the beauty. The songbook credits the
  paired tuning of Balinese gamelan and its name for the beating, ombak.
- **The islets** are treaties that almost work, each tuned in equal steps of another size:

| steps to the octave | what it gets right | what it costs |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | nothing clashes | no half steps, no leaning |
| 7 | every step the same size | no chord rings |
| 17 | fifths nearly pure, and a note between the notes | thirds either too wide or too narrow |
| 19 | minor thirds nearly pure | fifths a little flat |
| 24 | the piano's notes and the ones halfway between | the same thirds as the Seam |
| 31 | thirds and the low seventh nearly pure | fifths a little flat |
| 41 | fifths and sevenths nearly pure | so many notes that none feels like home |
| 53 | fifths and thirds within a hair | fifty-three notes |

## The Seam, as a city

- **It runs on beating.** Walkways, lifts, turnstiles and the clock are driven by fifths that are
  each two hundredths of a half step narrow. The Metronome hangs under the bridge and is driven the
  same way.
- **Everything is a little off and everything is connected.** Any key is a step from any other.
  Nothing rings.
- **The market** trades borrowed chords, and the caravan stops here every third morning.
- **The crack** is where the error that nobody shared collects. The Wolf lives in it. Every echo you
  turned away from is there.
- **The Grid** is the city made total: every day the same. Greys march out of it onto ground that
  has set grey.

## The sky

- **The way up** is a note that isn't on the wheel: the low seventh, traded by the caravan for
  something you let go.
- **A ladder of overtones.** Islands hang at the heights of the overtones of one low note. The gaps
  between rungs are an octave, a fifth, a fourth, a third, a smaller third, and on, each smaller
  than the last, for ever. Every interval in the world is somewhere on the ladder.
- **Higher: the planets.** Worlds that go round in step with each other, in the ratios of chords,
  slow enough to count.
- **Higher: the lighthouses.** Stars that turn hundreds of times a second and are, plainly, notes.
- **Higher: the spiral that closes.** Two heavy things falling round each other: a note that rises
  and rises, a ring, and then nothing. It is the one spiral in the world that closes, and it ends.
- **Higher: the beginning.** The first light has a note in it, with overtones.
- **At the top: light.** Hold an A and climb forty octaves, and it arrives as orange. Hold the
  garden's C forty-one, and it is the garden's green.

## The deep

- **The way down** is the Mirror Pool.
- **The world reflected.** Overtones run downward, so every major chord is a minor one. A door shut
  above is open in the mirror.
- **Lower, pitch becomes rhythm.** Below about twenty a second a note is a beat. Lower still it is a
  bar, and then a day. The thirteen mornings, heard from far enough down, are one rising tune.
- **Inside a petal.** At the smallest size things hold their shape by standing waves, and only
  certain shapes can stand.
- **At the bottom, a country with no octave.** Its scale is thirteen steps of three to one, and its
  voices have odd overtones only. Home never comes round again there. The songbook credits Heinz
  Bohlen and John Pierce.

## After the thirteenth morning

- **Each night:** up a fifth, down a fifth, or stay.
- **The sunflower.** In the thirteenth morning's garden one flower has come up that was not there
  before. Its seeds are set by a turn that agrees with no count at all, so no two ever line up, and
  the head is full.
- **The wheel gains a second direction,** by thirds, and later a third, by sevenths. Fifths go round
  the lake, thirds go out from it, sevenths go where the caravan comes from.
- **The lands out there are made, not built.** A land's place is three numbers: how many fifths,
  thirds and sevenths from the garden. Its tuning is pure. Its material follows whichever number is
  largest: fifths make stone and glass, thirds make leaf and water, sevenths make salt, rust and
  smoke. Rules and the three numbers make its ground, its things and its few lives. No two are the
  same, and none repeats.
- **Songs are places.** A phrase is a walk: each step in it is a fifth, a third or a seventh, up or
  down. Sing a phrase at the river and it carries you to the land where that walk ends. Anyone who
  sings the same phrase arrives at the same land, on any console.
- **Songs are seeds.** A plant in a garden has a note and a short tune. Sing that tune at another
  garden's soil and the plant grows there.
- **The long spiral.** Along the fifths, home keeps coming back and missing:

| fifths out | it comes back | two tones at middle C swell once in |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | 23.5 hundredths of a half step sharp: the thirteenth morning | 0.28 s |
| 41 | 19.8 flat | 0.34 s |
| 53 | 3.6 sharp | 1.8 s |
| 306 | 1.8 flat | 3.7 s |
| 665 | 0.08 sharp | 87 s |

  Each of the five is a garden, and each is different. Nothing is said at any of them.

## The calendar

- **The day** is about twenty minutes.
- **The turn** is twelve mornings, and it misses by a comma.
- **The moon** gains three hundredths of its cycle each morning, as the real moon gains on the
  months: there are 12.37 moons to a year. The first evening is dark of the moon. It is first full
  on the seventeenth morning. Twelve mornings on, it is not where it was. So no two turns have the
  same nights.
- **The tide** follows the moon: a little higher at new and at full.
- **Rounds.** Lives go round in 3, 4, 5 and 7 mornings. Three and four meet every 12, with five
  every 60, with seven every 420. The brood wakes every 13 and its cousin every 17; they wake
  together every 221.
- **Sped up, the calendar is a chord.** Rounds of 3, 4 and 5 mornings, heard fast enough, are a
  minor chord.
- **The almanac** is a page in the songbook: turn it to any morning and it shows the sun's land, the
  moon, and who is where.
- **The handheld's own clock is not used.** It doesn't count while the handheld is off (measured).
