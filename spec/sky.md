## The sky in one breath

The sky is one voice and the shape it makes in the air. The sun is a singer holding the day's note.
Over the lake its overtones stand as rings of light, one ring for each overtone, each higher and
smaller than the last, in the shape of a horn's bell with the lake as its mouth. The bright place in
the sun's voice climbs the rings all morning and comes down all afternoon, so the hour is a height,
a colour and a note. The stars are the lands past the lake, set out by the fifth's own turn. The
moon is yesterday, still sounding. Everything that flies is a wing beating at a rate, and the rate
is its note. From the lake's surface to light is forty-one octaves: 4.1 km, straight up.

One new number carries all of it: **height is the octave, and an octave is 100 m** (decided here, to
tune). It was chosen because it makes two things in the atlas literally true at once. The comma
becomes 1.96 m, which is the "step" the rim climbs to at the Seam. And "hold the garden's C
forty-one octaves" becomes a climb of 4.1 km.

## 1. The hand and the eye

**The first evening.** The world has been a page seen from above. It tilts back. The sky comes into
the frame from the top, and for one breath it is still night: a pinwheel of small coloured points,
each twinkling at its own slow rate. There is no moon. Then a thin green line lights along the whole
horizon, brightest at your own shore. A ring of light comes up out of it, perfectly round, and the
screen's own light rises with it. Above it a second green hoop lights, far out over the lake. Then a
yellow-green one, higher and smaller. Then green, then one thin red one, then yellow-green. Each
arrives with a note: the drone, then the five notes a bugle has, rising, in C. You have not touched
anything. The thrush that sings only those notes starts up in the asters. You feel that the day is
something being sung, and that you are in it.

**The fortieth day.** You wake and look at the lake before anything else. The sun is coming up
yellow over the Glass Hills, so it is a D day and the wind will be light. One pulsing point stands
over the Glass Hills with the sun: the caravan is there today. A cloud you helped fill sits low over
the Rain Steps, with a few pale blue figures drifting in it that you recognise. You run for the east
shore because the dragonfly's patrol passes the big reed two minutes after dawn. You catch it, and
from its back your one note rings things ahead of you a fifth higher than you can sing. At noon you
are on the sixth ring, 258 m over the water, looking at the dark ring above you, the one only the
caravan's note lights.

**The third year.** You do not look at the sky. You read it. The sun's colour says the hour. The
arms of the stars say how far you are along the spiral, and tonight the star you have been walking
toward for a year stands a hand's width from the garden's meridian. The sun comes up 35 m along the
shore from the first morning's mark, where a row of your own old dawns is printed on the ground. It
will rain tomorrow, because you let three days go this week. You hold a note at the foot of the
thread and ride it up for forty-eight seconds, because you like the part where the radio goes by.

## 2. What the sky is: the horn

**Sees, hears, does, feels.** From every shore, over the middle of the lake, hoops of light hang in
a stack. The lowest is the shore itself. The next is a hoop nearly two kilometres across, a hundred
metres up. They get smaller and closer together as they rise, until at noon they are a spire 400 m
high. Above it the thread is dark until someone sings up it. Each hoop is a ragged reef: seeds,
down, a feather, drops, spores, all hanging still in the air. Each sounds one note of the day's
chord, softly. You can stand on them while they sound.

**The law.** Law 4, standing waves. The sun's drone drives the air over the lake, and its overtones
stand in it. Things rest in the still places. This is real: small things hang in the quiet nodes of
a standing sound wave (acoustic levitation; general knowledge, to check). The reefs are what a
garden's air holds after a storm, at one to a hundred: a dandelion parachute is 1.4 m across
(Cummins and others, Nature 2018: 13.8 mm), a box elder key 3 to 4 m, a sparrow's feather 5 m.

- **Rung n hangs at 100 × log2(n) metres.** The gaps are an octave, a fifth, a fourth, a third, a
  smaller third, and on, for ever, as the atlas says.
- **Ring n is 1,850 ÷ n metres in radius.** Ring 1 is the lake's own shore. That makes the stack an
  exponential horn, which is the shape of a real horn's bell.
- **Ring n carries n waves round it,** so it has 2n still places and 2n bellies. The reef is
  thickest at a still place and thins toward each belly, so you stand at a still place and walk out
  along the thinning reef to be thrown. The sun drives the rings from where it rose, so ring 1's
  belly on the day's shore is always at the sunrise.
- **A belly throws you as high as its overtone is loud.** The sun's nth overtone is one nth as loud,
  so the throw is 144 m ÷ n (100 ÷ ln 2; to tune). The gap to the next rung is 100 × log2(1 + 1/n),
  which is always a little less. So every belly throws you exactly one rung and never two. It works
  because the loudness of the harmonic series falls at the same rate as its gaps close, so one throw
  fits every rung: any throw from 144 m ÷ n to just under 158.5 m ÷ n does it, and tuning must stay
  inside that.
- **Every seventh rung is dark.** The sun rises on the border between yesterday's land and today's,
  which is one seventh of the way along the day's seven lit lands. A string struck one seventh of
  the way along has no seventh overtone (`laws.md`, the families). So rungs 7, 14, 21, 28 and on
  never sound, never shine and hold nothing. From rung 6 the throw is 24 m. It carries you to 282 m,
  where rung 7 should be, and there is nothing there. Rung 8 is 41.5 m up. This is the atlas's gate,
  by law: "the way up is a note that isn't on the wheel". When you bring the caravan's seventh, the
  dark rings light for as long as you hold it, in chrome.

- **Colour.** A rung takes the hue of its note, by fifths, like everything else. A rung whose note
  is not on the wheel (11, 13, and the sevenths once lit) has no hue and is drawn in chrome.
- **The reefs let go at dusk.** When the sun's note dies the still places go with it, and everything
  they held comes down over the lake through the dusk, ringing where it lands. Every dawn the rings
  fill again with what the wind brings.
- **Plants are measured against it.** A 100 m aster tops out at rung 2, a 158 m one at rung 3, and a
  300 m reed at rung 8.

**The cheat:** floating platforms at hand-picked heights that are always solid, with a locked door
where the seventh should be.

## 3. The sun

**Sees, hears, does, feels.** The sun is not a disc. It is a figure of light, drawn the way every
voice is drawn: the figure of its note against your home. With home on the day's note it is a plain
ring. Put home a fifth under it and it becomes three lobes by two. In the dark moods it is a dense
rosette. In the Garden it stands dead still. At the Seam it turns once in 1.1 seconds. It sounds a
low held note, and over that a soft whistle that steps upward all morning. The backlight is its
loudness.

**The law.** The sun is a sung voice like the five who travel with you: a source through bottles
(`laws.md`). It sings every overtone but the sevenths, because it rises one seventh of the way along
the day's lit lands (section 2). Which overtones stand is set by height alone: every rung at or
below the sun sounds. Distance only makes a far sun softer and duller, as it does any voice (law 6).
That is real overtone singing, heard backwards: a whistle rising out of a drone (the Reed Mouths'
sixth wonder), with the sun's height as the mouth.

- **Where it rises.** On ring 1, at the border of yesterday's land and today's. "Every dawn in this
  world is yesterday settling into today."
- **How it climbs.** Up the horn's wall on the day's meridian, like a thrown ball: height 400 m ×
  sin(π × t ÷ 18 min). It reaches rung 16 at noon, 400 m up and 116 m from the axis, and comes down
  the same way. Eighteen minutes of daylight in the twenty is an estimate, to tune.
- **Every rung at or below the sun sounds. Every rung above it is silent, dark, and not there.**

| minute | the sun stands on | heard | seen |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | rung 1, the shore | the drone, with its octave faint over it: the speaker carries nothing below 400 Hz alone (`texture.md`) | the horizon ring |
| 1.5 | 2 | the octave | one hoop |
| 2.3, 3.0, 3.6, 4.0 | 3, 4, 5, 6 | fifth, octave, third, fifth: the arpeggio | the triad's three hues |
| 4.5 | 7 | nothing | a dark gap |
| 4.9 to 7.8 | 8 to 15 | a scale, a step every 22 to 33 seconds | five hues and two chrome rings |
| 9 | 16: noon | the top octave, for one instant; round it rung 15 is the top lit rung for two and a half minutes | every ring lit but the seventh and fourteenth, for that instant; the backlight at its highest |
| 9 to 18 | 16 back down to 1 | the same, falling | the sky closing from the top |

- **Dawn is an arpeggio by physics.** Rungs 2 to 6 are the five notes a bugle has, over the drone.
  Nobody wrote the morning call.
- **The backlight** follows the sun's loudness as heard where Hum stands: 14 of 255 in the first
  dark (as built), low at 2am, highest at noon.
- **Noon shimmer.** The sun warms pipes sharp, which the atlas already says of the Nozzle and of
  cold glass. Strings do not follow. So at noon pipes stand up to 3 cents above strings (to tune),
  and figures between them turn once a beat: once in 2.2 seconds at middle C, 1.7 times a second at
  1,000 a second, and faster higher up. At dawn and dusk they stand still, which is when the lands
  gather to sing. Morgan's ears decide if it stays.
- **Cost.** One of the sixteen sung voices. One figure, about 200 marks. The backlight as built.

**The cheat:** a sun sprite on a timer, with a tint that follows a clock.

## 4. Its colours, by law

Nothing here is chosen. A day's colours are the hues of the overtones the sun has opened so far.

**Through one day**

| hour | what is lit | why |
|---|---|---|
| 2am | black. Stars, wanderers, the moon in yesterday's hue, flyers by their own voices, your call's ring climbing | the sun is silent, and silence is dark |
| dawn | the horizon ring, then rungs 2 to 6: three hues | the sun stands on the low rungs |
| morning | the scale rungs add the ninth, the major seventh and two chrome rings | the sun climbs past them |
| noon | fourteen of the first sixteen rings, the sixteenth only at the instant of noon; every thing in the land lit faintly; overhead, the high overtones as a sparse field of fine points in the day's hue (chrome when the day's hue is red), never a wash | the sun stands at the top, and every rung below it but the sevenths sounds |
| afternoon | the sky closes from the top, back to the triad | the sun falls away |
| dusk | rungs 6 to 1, then the horizon ring alone, in the day's pure hue | the sun has come down to the lowest |

**Through the turn.** The day's hue walks the wheel. Each dawn is a triad, and a triad is a colour
scheme: the day's hue, the hue one step warmer (the fifth) and one far accent (the third, four steps
round).

| morning | key | root | fifth | third | what else the sky does |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | C | green | yellow-green | red | the sun stands still; no moon; almost no wind |
| 2 | G | yellow-green | yellow | rose | wind half again as fast |
| 3 | D | yellow | orange | purple | fifths stand; the third's ring shakes 16 times a second and holds no reef |
| 4 | A | orange | red | violet | the third's ring stands dead still; the fifth's turns |
| 5 | E | red | rose | blue | the sun is a red lamp over a land that hardly answers it |
| 6 | B | rose | purple | azure | seen through a windscreen |
| 7 | F♯ | purple | violet | cyan | the sun turns once in 1.1 s |
| 8 | D♭ | violet | blue | teal | the first doubled sun: half a second; almost no wind, and none under the dome |
| 9 | A♭ | blue | azure | **green: the garden's** | the third's ring is home's colour; half moon |
| 10 | E♭ | azure | cyan | yellow-green | the cloud; rain |
| 11 | B♭ | cyan | teal | yellow | the hardest wind of the way home |
| 12 | F | teal | **green: the garden's** | orange | doubled for three seconds; moon 76% lit |
| 13 | B♯ | green, doubled | | | two suns that don't agree |

- The ninth morning's third and the twelfth morning's fifth are the garden's green because C is the
  third of A♭ and the fifth of F. `the-way-home.md` has the vine singing "green: the garden's" on
  the ninth. The sky was already saying it.
- **Red.** E is red by the wheel. The style book says red is found light, never a fill. Decided: sky
  red is only ever a thin line or the sun's own figure, with no halo, no wash and no afterglow. On
  the fifth morning that is the whole sun. Tarmac and salt take almost nothing (law 1's material
  term), so the Highway under its own noon is as dim as other lands' dusk, and the sun is one red
  lamp over black flats, kin to the tail lights. If Morgan reads the rule more strictly, E's rings
  go chrome and only the sun's figure keeps red.

**The way home.** From the eighth dawn the sun rises as two figures a comma apart: 1.96 m apart in
height, less than a dot from any shore, so there the pair is seen by its beat: the sun's figure
turns and every ring breathes at the beat rate. Only within about 250 m, on a ring, do the two
copies stand visibly apart (worked out from a 55-degree view on 480 dots). Every ring of the horn is
doubled by the same 1.96 m, and each pair beats. The land gives way and they close, in half a second
on the eighth and three seconds on the twelfth, as `the-way-home.md` has it.

- **The backlight shows the beat, safely.** The sun's drone is the day's note itself, middle C on
  the garden's morning (261.63 Hz, `laws.md`), where a comma beats 3.57 times a second. That is too
  fast for the eyes, so the backlight swells once for every two beats, 1.78 times a second, and
  never moves more than a tenth of its range in one swell. That keeps it under the web's standard
  limit of three flashes a second (WCAG 2.3.1). Morgan's eyes decide.

**The thirteenth morning.** The sun comes up 7.04 degrees further round than it did on the first
morning. That is a comma as an angle, and on the shore it is **227 m**. You can pace it:
thirty-eight seconds at a run from the first morning's mark, which the ground has kept. Two suns
rise, 227 m apart along the Garden's shore and 1.96 m apart in height, and every ring is double.

- **The clap:** the pairs snap to one. Every ring shifts a hair to an equal step. The rings are grey
  and drawn in pixel stairs. The stars' arms straighten into twelve dead spokes and stop rippling.
- **The step:** the sun goes on round, 227 m a turn, and the mouth of the horn narrows as the lake
  drains.
- **The hold:** both stay.

**After.**

- **Tomorrow is a rung of today.** Up a fifth is rung 3. By thirds is rung 5. By sevenths is rung 7.
  At the river those rungs brighten as each choice is sung ahead. All through the first turn,
  tomorrow's sun has been hanging in today's sky as the first ring that is not the day's own colour.
- **The sunrise walks the shore.** Each dawn leaves its first light on the ground where it rose, in
  its hue (`size.md`: the ground remembers). Over years the shore carries a row of your dawns.
  Fifty-three fifths out, the sun rises 35 m from the first mark. Forty-one out, 192 m short of it.
  At 306, 17 m short. At 665, **0.73 m**: within a pace.
- **At the 665th garden** the two suns swell together once in 87 seconds, and the screen's own light
  breathes with them.

**The cheat:** a sky gradient per land and per hour, picked to look nice.

## 5. The lit arc: a day side and a night side

**Sees, hears, does, feels.** From any shore, seven lands in a row are in daylight and five are
dark, with only their own voices showing across the water. The terminator moves one land a morning.
The sun's minute is the same everywhere. How much of its light reaches a land is not. Yesterday's
land, today's and the five ahead are lit as the sun climbs, each fainter the further along the
fifths it stands, so all day the land five ahead looks like first light. The other five stay as dark
as 2am all day and show only their own voices. To be in the night, you sail to it.

**The law.** Law 1, with the canon's wheel on top. The sun's own threes reach forward: its 3rd, 9th,
27th, 81st and 243rd overtones are the notes of the next five lands, each about a third as bright as
the last (to tune). The land behind rings because the sun's note is its own third overtone. Two
lands a third round each way glow faintly warm through the fifth overtone, which is why the vine's
three lands are never fully dark to each other. Each land's brightness is twelve sums at dawn from
its own tuning, so pure lands ring and the Seam is never bright and never black. This is "the wheel
is a sky" (`the-wheel.md`), laid on the ground.

- **Dawn travels at the speed of sound.** The sun's first light runs out from its rising point as a
  ring, like a call. It crosses the lake in 62 seconds and the whole world in 95. On the first
  morning dawn is at your feet. On the seventh you see the sun on the far shore, and a minute later
  the line of answering dew reaches you, with a wave of small high ticks.
- **Every place's two faces** (the atlas) are now somewhere you can go on any morning.
- **Cost.** The far lands are already a few hundred lines each, always loaded. This sets their
  brightness from twelve numbers.

**The cheat:** one global time of day.

## 6. The moon

**Sees, hears, does, feels.** A thin arc in yesterday's colour, over a land ahead of the sun. It
grows all through the first turn. Under it at night there is a soft drone a fifth below today's
note. Sing a phrase at it and the phrase comes back, softer and duller, on the beat, and the moon
flushes with your colour as it does.

**The law.**

- **The moon is yesterday, still sounding.** Its note is a fifth below the day's, so it rides
  yesterday's ladder, a fourth above today's rungs, at 241.5 m over its land (to tune). Its hue is
  yesterday's. On the first evening there is no moon because there is no yesterday.
- **Night is the chord before home.** The moon's note is the root of the amen. When the sun comes
  up, the amen lands. That is the atlas's ninth Porch wonder, with a body.
- **The tide.** Today's note is the moon's third overtone, so the lake belongs to the moon and falls
  toward it at a third of full strength (law 2).
- **It is an echo wall** (law 6). It sends back what reaches it, as much as it is lit. Under it the
  return is 8 seconds, which is four bars at 120. From the next land it is about 34 seconds. From
  across the lake it is two minutes. So you walk to set the length of the loop, and the moon, from
  across the lake, is the longest looper in the first turn.
- **Its ladder holds reefs at night,** as strongly as it is lit. On a bright-moon night you can
  climb.
- **The calendar is a temperament, with real numbers.** A year has 12.368266 moons (365.2422 ÷
  29.530589 days), so the moon gains 0.030689 of its cycle each morning and comes round in 32.585
  mornings. It stands ahead of the sun by its phase: twelve wedges to a cycle.

| morning | lit | the moon stands |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | with the sun: dark |
| 5 | 14% | a wedge and a half ahead |
| 9 | 49% | three wedges ahead |
| 12 | 76% | four ahead |
| 13 | 84% | four and a half ahead |
| 17 | full | straight across the lake from the sun |
| 34 | new | with the sun again |

- **The first turn's nights grow brighter all the way home, and the moon is not yet full when you
  arrive.** The first full moon is the seventeenth morning, which is the morning the Cousins first
  wake.
- **The moon's own long spiral:** it nearly comes back after 3 turns (a tenth of a cycle off), 8
  turns (0.054), 19 turns (0.003), and 334 turns (0.001). Nineteen is Meton's cycle of 432 BC: 235
  moons in 19 years, to within two hours. Real calendars close that gap by adding a thirteenth moon
  to seven years in nineteen, which is the Seam's treaty again. The songbook should credit Meton and
  the Babylonian, Hebrew and Chinese calendars that keep the same count (to check).

**The cheat:** a moon texture with a phase from the system clock.

## 7. The stars and the wanderers

**Sees, hears, does, feels.** At night, lean back. The stars are a pinwheel: fifty-three fine arms
near the horizon, each star along them a different colour; higher up they gather into twelve, each
bending slowly clockwise. Every star twinkles, and stars of two colours twinkle against each other:
three for two between a fifth. Along each arm a slow ripple runs. Call, and about a minute later the
stars kin to your home flare together. That is a different constellation for every home.

**The law.**

- **A star is a land past the lake.** Star n is the land n pure fifths from the garden. It stands at
  n × 210.59 degrees round the horizon, which is the fifth's true share of the octave as an angle.
  It stands higher the further out it is, on an even spread: 90° × √(1 − n ÷ 665) from the zenith.
  The first twelve are the lands themselves, set round the sky by pitch, not by the lake: G stands
  seven twelfths of the way round from C, not one. Over each land an even number of fifths from the
  Garden stands its own colour, and over each of the other six stands the colour of the land
  straight across.
- **The arms are the near-misses, drawn.** Twelve fifths is a full turn and 7.04 degrees more, so
  each arm leans. The fifth's turn nearly agrees with 12, then 41, then 53, then 306, then 665,
  which is the atlas's long spiral. So the sky shows fifty-three fine arms low, each star along them
  a different colour, and from about 40 degrees up twelve arms. Round the zenith they give way to
  seven and five. The far gardens are the stars that fall nearly on the garden's meridian. The 665th
  is at the zenith, 0.02 degrees from true.
- **The sunflower is its twin.** The thirteenth garden's sunflower is the same construction with a
  turn that agrees with nothing, so it has no arms. Look from one to the other and you see what a
  comma is.
- **A star turns hundreds of times a second and is, plainly, a note** (the atlas's lighthouses). Its
  turning is its land's key. It twinkles at that note eight octaves down, by the rule already in
  `laws.md`: C once a second, G one and a half times.
- **The ripple is the comma.** Two neighbours on an arm are a comma apart, so their twinkles drift
  and meet again every 72 seconds on the garden's arm, and as often as every 38 seconds on B's.
- **Lands by thirds and sevenths** come out as companion stars after the ending. A third reached by
  four fifths and the pure third are 21.5 cents apart, so they stand 6.45 degrees apart: a double
  star. Every close pair in the sky is a comma.
- **Songs are constellations.** A phrase is a walk of fifths, thirds and sevenths (the atlas). Sung
  at the river, it lights its path from star to star, and the last one is where you wake.
- **The wanderers are the rounds.** Six points move against the stars, one notch a morning each:
  rounds of 3, 4, 5, 7, 13 and 17 mornings. The three stands with the sun whenever the caravan
  shares your sun. The four does when the vine does. Each pulses at its round twelve octaves up:
  1.14, 0.85, 0.68, 0.49, 0.26 and 0.20 times a second. So the caravan's pulses four for the vine's
  three, and the first three together are a slow minor chord in light. These are the atlas's "worlds
  that go round in step, in the ratios of chords, slow enough to count". When wanderers line up it
  is a gathering (`years.md`). **The almanac page is a drawing of this sky for any morning.**
- **Real twin:** the seven planets of TRAPPIST-1 keep periods near 8:5, 5:3, 3:2, 3:2, 4:3 and 3:2
  (measured: 1.603, 1.672, 1.506, 1.509, 1.342, 1.519). The pairs are a little off, and resonances
  of three link the whole chain. What two can't close, a third can.
- **Cost.** 665 stars now and up to about 2,000 with companions. Each is one short line, placed and
  twinkled in the vertex shader from the sample count, so the world's step pays nothing. Six
  wanderers, about 100 lines. No new pass.

**The cheat:** random dots that fade in at night.

## 8. Wind, cloud, rain, thunder

**Wind**

- **Sees, hears, does, feels.** You never see wind. You see what it plays: a wave of light crossing
  the alfalfa, a wire starting to hum, a gust coming as a line of stalks lighting one after another.
  It always comes from the land the sun is over.
- **The law.** Wind is the sun's breath: a steady stream, which the engine already has as a way to
  excite a thing. A wire sings at 0.2 × the wind's speed ÷ its width (the real Strouhal rule, and
  the Alfalfa's ninth wonder). So a wind a fifth higher is half again as fast. The wind's speed is
  the day's note, folded into one octave: slowest on C and D♭, fastest on B♭ and B. The Garden after
  the storm is nearly still. The Aster Steps, which lend stillness, are next stillest. The Reed
  Mouths, where wind pushes you off your note, are nearly the windiest. None of that was arranged.
- **Sailing the air.** The glide is a sail, and the atlas's sailing rule holds in the air. With home
  on the wind's note you hang still. A fifth off you run. Straight across you are thrown back. In
  wonder (Lift) you settle onto the nearest still place. **Thumbs:** the glide is latched by a tap
  of the bottom face. The left thumb steers. The right index holds R2. The right thumb nudges home
  on the downbeat. Nothing needs a face button and the right stick together.

**Cloud**

- **Sees, hears, does, feels.** One cloud stands over the Rain Steps and is seen from every shore.
  Each take you let go rises from the lake at night as a thin blue thread and joins it. Faint flat
  blue figures drift in it: your released days. The fuller it is, the lower it hangs.
- **The law.** A cloud is a crowd of drops, and each drop is a tiny bubble-like resonator (the
  Garden's ninth wonder). A crowd of every size answers every note a little and holds it a long
  time. It is the Cistern, in the air. So a cloud is the sky's memory, as the lake's level is the
  world's.
- **Weather coming** is read from its height. After the tenth morning it rains again whenever enough
  has been let go. Letting go makes weather, and weather raises the lake.

**Rain**

- **Sees, hears, does, feels.** Falling rain is invisible. You see only where it lands: a spark on
  each thing, and a ring only on the nearest few dozen, in that thing's own colour and note. So rain
  plays the land: glass in the Glass Hills, bells in the Bell Meadow, drums on mushroom caps, a hiss
  of sparks on the lake. Call in the rain, and for one instant your ring lights a dome of drops
  hanging round you, each 10 to 50 cm across. You see the rain's edge coming over the lake as a line
  of sparks.
- **The law.** A falling drop makes no sound (the Garden's ninth wonder), and silence is dark. A
  landing is a strike. A call's ring lights what it passes (law 6). On the tenth morning each drop
  carries a note of a take you let go, so the rain plays your old songs back, scattered.

**Thunder and lightning**

- **Sees, hears, does, feels.** One jagged line of light, the beam's single fast stroke, from the
  cloud to something tall. Whatever it hits flashes every one of its colours at once and then
  settles into its own. You count: a crack, then a roll that goes lower and longer.
- **The law.** Law 6. The thunder is made from the picture of the bolt. Each piece of the drawn line
  sends one impulse. It arrives after its distance at 60 m a second, softer and duller the further
  it came. So the near end cracks, the far end rumbles, and the roll lasts as long as the bolt
  reaches away from you: a 600 m bolt pointing your way rolls for ten seconds, and seen side-on from
  far off it rolls for one. A kilometre is 16.7 seconds, about eight bars. Lightning falls by law 2,
  toward the tall thing that holds home's note, so you can tell where it will land. An impulse has
  every overtone, so a struck thing shows its whole self. The ground keeps the scar.
- **Borrowed weather** (the atlas): reach under the horizon for G and wind comes for a phrase. E♭
  brings rain. E brings 2am. F brings evening. D♭ brings stillness.
- **Cost.** A cloud is 2,000 to 4,000 short lines (to be measured). Rain is up to about 500 sparks
  and no falling geometry. A bolt is 40 to 120 lines for a few frames, and its thunder is that many
  impulses into about two dozen low resonators.

**The cheat:** a rain overlay with a looped sample, and thunder at a random delay after a white
flash.

## 9. Who lives up there

**One law for everything with wings.** A flyer is a pendulum, and for the small ones a resonator. A
real insect's flight muscle drives its thorax at the thorax's own rate, which is law 7 in a body.

- **Size is pitch.** Big wings beat time and small wings sing. A swallow's wings are sixteenth
  notes. A dragonfly's, at about 30 a second, purr on the line where rhythm turns into pitch. A bee
  is a note. A midge is a whine. A flyer's register is its real wingbeat, and its note within that
  is its home land's, so it is always in tune. Bird wings lock to the land's beat (law 3).
- **Speed is an interval.** A flyer cruises at a fraction of the speed of sound that bends its note
  cleanly (law 6), so each kind is known by ear first by its own sound, a fly's buzz, a bee's hum, a
  dragonfly's purr, a swallow's twitter, and then by how far its note falls as it passes: a third
  for a fly, a fifth for a bee, an octave for a dragonfly, a twelfth for a swallow.
- **The wing draws the body.** A hovering wingtip really traces a figure of eight: two against one,
  the octave's figure. So a hoverer is drawn as two glowing eights. A cruiser's wingtips write its
  wave along its path in the afterglow: speed ÷ wingbeat is the wavelength. Insects leave a ribbon
  whose fine ripple is their timbre. Birds leave a wave you can count. With the lake full, the sky
  keeps these traces longer.
- **The wheel decides what each is to you today** (law 2). On your wedge it flies with you and keeps
  your time. A fifth above, it comes down and settles beside you. A fifth below, you rise to it. A
  third round, it warms and sings. Straight across, it throws you, and a thrown Hum can be thrown on
  purpose. The same dragonfly is your ferry one morning and out of reach the next. No list says so.
- **Nothing dies.** What a hunter takes becomes its light: it is brighter by evening, and its line
  carries the notes it gathered.
- **Riding.** Tap the top face when it has settled. It keeps its own round. You sing and look. Jump
  to leave, and tap again to glide.

## 11. The sky as a place

**How it is reached**

- **Riding the dawn** (the first turn, any morning). Be on the shore of the day's land at sunrise.
  Open the glide in the lift of the first ring. The wind blows from that land out over the water,
  along the sun's own meridian. It is 925 m to the second ring, and the sun gets there in a minute
  and a half. Each ring throws you to the next as the sun lights it. The first legs are long sails
  over open water toward a figure of light. The last are a stair winding round a spire. You reach
  rung 6 four minutes after dawn, and the dark ring is over your head. Timing and one choice: home a
  fifth off the day's note before you leap, so the wind runs you out; with home on its note you
  would hang still.
- **By wing:** the dragonfly passes rings 2 to 4, the swallows reach 8, and a sparrow's throw
  reaches a crown.
- **Through the seventh:** with the caravan's note the dark rings hold while you hold it, and you
  can follow the sun to rung 16 by noon.
- **Thumbs:** the seventh, once traded, is chosen before the climb and held on R2 by the right index
  like any note, with no stick held. The right thumb jumps and opens the glide on the bottom face,
  and the left thumb steers. Nothing needs the right stick and a face button together.
- **Leaving.** The sky closes from the top all afternoon. A rung goes dark when the sun falls past
  it, and you glide. The sun sets on you.
- **Breath.** What carries Hum in the sky costs no breath once it has begun: a glide opened in a
  ring's lift or in the dawn wind, a note held to ride the thread, and the caravan's seventh held
  over a dark rung each cost one home of breath to begin (to tune) and nothing after. The sky
  carries them, not Hum. A glide anywhere else costs breath as `../SPEC.md` section 9 says.
- **Above rung 16** nothing sounds unless you make it sound. After the ending you sing a rung's note
  and it lights and holds, your band keeps the lower ones lit, and a tune of rising rungs is a stair
  you build as you go. Or you climb on a bright-moon night.

**The thread.** Above 1.2 km the rings are narrower than Hum, and the horn is a single line of
light. Hold a note at its foot and ride your own ring up it at the speed of sound: 1.67 seconds an
octave, **68 seconds from the lake to light**. Each storey is what really has that frequency, and
what is there is heard brought down by whole octaves into the speaker's band, as a radio brings its
band down to hearing. Your own note is not brought down: it leaves hearing at about 500 m. Light is
sound forty octaves up, so radio is sound eleven to nineteen octaves up, and warmth thirty-six.

- With your own voice, the point slides along the line as you sing. It goes dark off either end.

## 13. The sky as a promise

- **The moment.** You look up and the day is a chord you can see being sung, in colours nobody
  picked. You go up into it by being in the right place at dawn, and it lets you down when the song
  ends.
- **Far.** From the first evening: the horizon ring, the hoops over the lake, the sun's figure, the
  pinwheel of stars. From every shore and every crown the horn is in sight. Deep in a land it is
  often hidden, and then the hour is read from the light on things and the screen's own light, which
  is the sun's loudness.
- **At its foot.** The day's shore at sunrise. The first hoop is 1.85 km across and a hundred metres
  up, and you are one metre tall. Your call takes about 15 seconds to cross the water to it (930 m
  at 60 m a second); from the middle of the lake a held note rides to the top in 68 seconds.
- **Inside.** The reefs under your feet. The throw. The dark ring over your head. The thread, the
  dial going by, the long dark, the bow.
- **After.** You tell the hour by a colour. You know which land is in night. You know the 227
  metres. And you look at a real dawn, a real moon and a real rainbow differently, because all three
  numbers were real.
- **A cheat would be:** a sky dome with a gradient, a sun sprite on a timer, random stars, a moon
  phase from a clock, cloud decals, a rain overlay, and birds as particles, with a "sky level" of
  floating platforms unlocked by a key item.
- **Full size, as measures.**
  - Every sky hue is worked out from a note. A test recolours the whole sky from one frequency.
  - A test shows a belly throws one rung and not two, for every n to 256.
  - The thunder is made from the bolt's lines. A test moves the bolt and the roll changes.
  - The star table matches n × 210.59 degrees.
  - The moon is new on morning 1 and full on morning 17.
  - The scripted player rides a dawn from the shore to rung 6 by timing and home a fifth off.
  - Frames at dawn, noon, dusk and 2am from all twelve lands are looked at.
  - The sky draws inside three passes and 30,000 lines, on the purple.
- **Asks:** Did you ever stop walking to look up?
